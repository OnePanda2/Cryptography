# 06 — Design Philosophies of Major Designers

*Part of a multi-file report — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map. Assumes `01`–`05`. This file draws on each designer's own public design-rationale writing, talks, and technical commentary rather than secondary characterization — where a claim below is an inference from the pattern of their published work rather than something they've stated explicitly, it's flagged as such.*

## Daniel J. Bernstein

**Stated priorities, consistently, across three decades of public design notes**: speed, implementation-safety (eliminating entire classes of implementation error by construction, not just by careful coding), and transparent, reproducible parameter generation. **The recurring pattern in his design rationale**: he tends to frame a new design's justification not as "this is elegant" but as "this specific implementation failure mode, which is real and has caused real problems, is now structurally impossible" — Curve25519's complete-addition-formula design (companion history report `02`) is argued for on exactly these grounds, and Salsa20's cache-timing motivation (this report's `05`) is the same pattern applied to side channels. **Where this shows up in the design process this report reconstructs**: Bernstein's designs consistently show the requirements-engineering stage (`01` §4) done unusually explicitly and publicly, with the design's justification written *as* a response to a named, specific failure mode rather than a general security argument — a designer reading his work primarily for the round-function mathematics will miss that a large fraction of his actual contribution, in his own framing, is at the requirements and architecture-choice layer (`01`), not the round-function layer.

**A genuinely distinguishing trait**: sustained skepticism, expressed publicly and repeatedly, toward closed or insufficiently-transparent standardization processes specifically — his public commentary on NIST elliptic curve parameter generation predates and directly anticipates the provenance-trust reckoning this conversation's companion history report traces to 2013 (`03`), and Curve25519's "nothing up my sleeve" generation reads, in retrospect, as a design choice made partly *in anticipation* of exactly the trust dynamic that later became field-wide consensus. **Where this creates friction with other philosophies profiled below**: this consistent institutional skepticism sits in some tension with Daemen and Rijmen's more *actively participatory* relationship with NIST-style open competitions (below) — both positions are compatible with valuing transparency, but they differ on whether the right response to a distrusted institution is to work *within* its competition processes (Daemen/Rijmen's consistent choice) or to build and popularize alternatives *outside* them first, letting adoption pressure the institutions later (closer to Bernstein's actual track record with Curve25519/Ed25519 preceding, rather than following, formal NIST-equivalent standardization).

## Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen

Profiled together because their major contributions (Rijndael/AES, and — with Bertoni, Peeters, Van Assche for Daemen specifically — Keccak/SHA-3) share a distinctive, jointly-articulated methodological signature: **security margin should be provable, via an explicit, general-purpose proof technique, not merely tested for.** The wide-trail strategy (companion history report `01`) is not just a property Rijndael happens to have — it's a reusable *methodology* Daemen and Rijmen explicitly developed and named, intended from the outset to outlive the specific cipher and be applicable to future designs (a purpose it has served, directly, via its 2020 ARX adaptation into the long-trail strategy, `03`–`05` of this report). **The recurring pattern in their design rationale, distinctively systematic relative to other designers profiled here**: their published design documentation (*The Design of Rijndael*, and Keccak's accompanying theoretical papers) reads less like a narrative justification and more like a mathematical construction with each step's necessity argued from first principles — a designer studying their published rationale is being taught a *proof technique* as much as a specific cipher, consistent with `01` §3's observation that the field's actual reward structure favors novelty concentrated in a transferable methodology over novelty in the specific artifact.

**Where they differ from each other, to the (limited) extent the historical record distinguishes them individually rather than as a pair**: Daemen's continuing, decades-spanning involvement across AES, Keccak, and the Xoodoo/Xoodyak lightweight-cryptography line (`05`) suggests a sustained personal research program specifically around permutation-based and sponge/duplex construction theory, extending well beyond the AES collaboration; Rijmen's visible subsequent work has ranged more broadly across cryptanalysis and cipher design generally. **Where they converge with Rogaway (below), distinctively**: both design traditions treat "can this be proven, not just tested" as the central question separating a mature design from an immature one — Daemen/Rijmen apply this at the primitive-design level (wide-trail/long-trail bounds), Rogaway applies it at the protocol/mode-of-composition level (below); the two are complementary halves of the same underlying value, applied at different layers of the stack.

## Phillip Rogaway

**Stated priorities**: rigor in exactly the sense that matters for *composability* — Rogaway's major contributions (the random oracle model with Bellare, OAEP, PSS, and the Encrypt-then-MAC generic-composition result, all companion history report `01`–`02`) are, almost without exception, about giving the field precise, checkable *definitions* of what a construction promises, specifically so that promise can be composed safely with other constructions' promises without hidden interaction effects — the Encrypt-then-MAC result (companion history report `06`'s AEAD thread) is exactly this pattern: not a new cipher, but a precise theorem about which compositions of already-existing primitives are safe and which aren't. **Where this shows up in this report's process framework**: Rogaway's contributions live almost entirely at `03`'s evaluation-ladder rungs 4–5 (formal security argument, concrete-security derivation) rather than at `02`'s round-function-invention or `03` §1's rotation-constant-search stages — a genuinely different center of gravity from Bernstein's or Daemen/Rijmen's work, and a useful reminder that "designing a cryptographic primitive" and "designing the formal framework that lets primitives be composed safely" are both legitimate, both essential, and both represented among the major contributors this report profiles, without being the same activity.

**A distinguishing trait not shared by the other designers profiled here**: sustained, explicit public writing on cryptography's *social and ethical* responsibilities — his essay on the moral character of cryptographic work is widely cited within the field as a rare instance of a leading technical researcher writing directly and seriously about whether specific research directions (mass-surveillance-enabling cryptanalysis, in particular) should be pursued at all, independent of their technical merit. **Why this belongs in a report about design philosophy rather than being an unrelated aside**: it reflects a view, expressed less explicitly but not contradicted by any other designer profiled here, that requirements engineering (`01` §4) should include an ethical dimension — not just "what threat model am I defending against" but "what is this specific technical capability actually for, and should it exist" — a question this report's process framework, focused on technical design discipline, has not itself asked, and which Rogaway's body of work suggests deserves a place in a complete account of how a thoughtful designer actually reasons.

## Jean-Philippe Aumasson

**Stated priorities, and a genuinely distinctive breadth**: across BLAKE/BLAKE2/BLAKE3, SipHash, NORX, and Ascon (as a co-author), Aumasson's design record spans more of the architecture-choice decision tree from `01` §5 than any other single designer profiled in this report — ARX (BLAKE-family, SipHash), ARX-based sponge/duplex (NORX), and SPN-sponge hybrid (Ascon), across genuinely different target requirements (general-purpose hashing, hash-table-flooding resistance, parallel AEAD, lightweight embedded devices). **What this breadth suggests about his design philosophy, as an inference from the pattern rather than a stated position**: a consistent willingness to let the requirements (`01` §4) determine the architecture fresh each time, rather than favoring one architecture family as a default and reaching for it regardless of fit — directly consistent with, and arguably the clearest applied demonstration of, `01` §5's decision-procedure framing, that architecture choice should follow from requirements rather than personal preference. **His book *Serious Cryptography*** (profiled in the companion general-cryptography report's Part `06` §4 as a recommended intermediate text) reflects the same instinct applied to teaching rather than design — an explicit, stated preference for clarity and directness over either oversimplification or unnecessary formalism, positioning him, among the designers profiled here, as the one whose public output most directly bridges the practitioner and researcher audiences this entire conversation's report series has had to navigate between.

**Where he converges with Bernstein specifically**: both consistently prioritize practical deployability and real-world performance as first-class design constraints, not afterthoughts subordinate to theoretical elegance — visible in SipHash's narrowly-scoped, aggressively-practical design brief (`05`) and echoed in Ascon's competition-winning combination of footprint, side-channel posture, and implementation maturity over Sparkle's more purely mathematical elegance (`05`'s Ascon case study). **Where he differs from Daemen/Rijmen's more singularly-focused, decades-long permutation-theory research program**: his portfolio reads as more opportunistically responsive to whatever specific competition or need arose at a given moment (CAESAR's call producing NORX, NIST LWC's call producing Ascon-adjacent work) than as a single sustained theoretical research program — a legitimate, different, and clearly also highly successful way of engaging with the field's competition-driven institutional structure (companion history report `08` §1.1).

## Thomas Pornin

**Stated priorities**: precision and correctness at the implementation layer specifically, with a consistent, almost severe emphasis on constant-time discipline and minimizing the attack surface a specification leaves open to implementer error — his extensive public technical writing (including a large, widely-cited body of detailed cryptography answers on public technical forums, and his BearSSL implementation work) consistently prioritizes closing the gap between "the specification is secure" and "any reasonable implementation of the specification is also secure," a distinction this report's `04` §1's designer-side reviewer-anticipation framing treats as important but which Pornin's public work treats as close to the *central* question. **Where this shows up in this report's process framework, distinctively**: while every other designer profiled here contributes primarily at the architecture-choice, round-function-invention, or formal-proof layers (`01`–`03`), Pornin's most visible, sustained contribution sits closest to companion ARX report `07`'s implementation-security material specifically — constant-time programming discipline, careful handling of edge cases in elliptic-curve arithmetic, and rigorous, precise technical exposition of exactly *why* a given implementation choice is or isn't safe. **What this suggests, as a closing point for this section**: the six designers profiled in this file collectively span nearly the entire process this report has reconstructed, from Bernstein's and Aumasson's requirements-and-architecture-layer work through Daemen/Rijmen's round-function-and-proof-methodology layer through Rogaway's composition-and-formal-framework layer to Pornin's implementation-correctness layer — no single one of them, examined alone, demonstrates the *complete* process this report describes, and a designer aspiring to work across the full process (as your original prompt's stated ultimate goal implies) should expect, honestly, to need fluency closer to all six of these perspectives combined than to any single one of them individually.

## Where They Agree, Stated Directly

Despite the real differences traced above, every designer profiled in this file converges, without exception, on: **novelty should be scarce and deliberate, not abundant** (`01` §3); **transparency and reproducibility in design choices (parameter generation, round-constant derivation) are non-negotiable**, not a nice-to-have (a consensus this conversation's companion history report `03`, `07` §1.5 traces hardening field-wide after 2013, but which several of these designers' own individual track records — Bernstein's and Daemen/Rijmen's especially — anticipated well before that consensus fully formed); and **a design's actual maturity is demonstrated through surviving external, adversarial scrutiny, not asserted through internal confidence** (`03` §3.4) — a genuinely unanimous position across six otherwise quite differently-styled research careers, and, per this report's Executive Summary, probably the single most load-bearing, most transferable lesson in this entire report if you take away only one thing.
