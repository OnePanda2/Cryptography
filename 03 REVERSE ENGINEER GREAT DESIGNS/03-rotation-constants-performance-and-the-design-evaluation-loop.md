## 1. Rotation Constant Design — The Actual Process, Not Just the Techniques

Your companion ARX-specific report (`04` §1) covers *what* rotation-constant search techniques exist (exhaustive, MILP-assisted, SAT-assisted, evolutionary). This section reconstructs the actual *sequence of judgment calls* a designer makes using those techniques — the process, not the toolbox.

### 1.1 Step one: decide what you're actually optimizing for, and in what order

Every reconstructable rotation-constant search proceeds against a stated *ranked* set of criteria, not a single metric — because, as companion ARX report `04` §1.4 makes explicit, a good rotation schedule must simultaneously deliver fast diffusion *and* resist rotational/RX-symmetry exploitation, and these two goals don't always point the same direction (a schedule optimized purely for fastest diffusion might, in principle, retain more exploitable symmetry than one that sacrifices a little diffusion speed for asymmetry). The reconstructable pattern across ChaCha, Salsa20, BLAKE2, and Sparkle's published rationale: **diffusion speed is optimized first, as the primary objective, with rotational/RX-resistance applied as a filtering constraint on the candidate set diffusion-speed optimization produces**, not as a co-equal objective optimized jointly from the start. This ordering is a real, specific design choice, not an accident — it reflects that diffusion speed is cheap to estimate early (a quick script, per `02` §2.1's second elimination pass) while rotational/RX-resistance checking is comparatively more expensive to verify rigorously (needing the automated RX-search tooling covered in companion ARX report `04` §2.4), so the cheap, primary-objective search happens first and narrows the field before the expensive constraint-check is applied.

### 1.2 Step two: exploit symmetry-awareness to shrink the search space before searching

A designer who understands the equivalence classes described in companion ARX report `04` §1.3 (word-reversal symmetry, rotation-amount complementation, common-factor-with-word-size redundancy) doesn't search the full naive space of rotation-constant combinations — they **prune it first**, discarding entire equivalence classes of candidates before running any expensive evaluation. This is a genuinely important, under-taught design skill: the difference between a tractable rotation-constant search and an intractable one is often exactly this pruning step, done by hand, before any automated tool is invoked at all. ChaCha's specific schedule (16, 12, 8, 7) reads, once you know this, as the survivor of exactly this kind of pruned search — four distinct values, none sharing an obvious common factor with 32, none an obvious complement or reversal of another in the schedule — evidence (though not, absent Bernstein's own explicit confirmation, proof) that this pruning logic was applied rather than the schedule being an arbitrary choice that happened to work.

### 1.3 Step three: escalate from hand-reasoning to automated search as candidates survive

Directly mirroring `02` §2.1's round-function elimination-pass logic, rotation-constant search escalates in expense: hand-reasoning and quick diffusion-speed scripts eliminate the bulk of candidates cheaply; only survivors receive full MILP/SAT-assisted optimal-trail evaluation (companion ARX report `05` §1) and, per current (2023–2025) best practice, explicit RX-cryptanalysis checking (companion ARX report `04` §2.4, §1.5) as a *separate*, not-subsumed-by-differential-search, validation step. **The Window Heuristic's 2025 partial-linearization advance** (companion ARX report `05` §1.6) is directly relevant here as a process point, not just a technical one: it exists specifically because even the "automated search" stage of this process was, before 2025, more expensive than it needed to be for ARX's addition operator specifically, and a genuine chunk of current rotation-constant-search research is about making this *specific* stage of the process cheaper and more exhaustive, not about inventing new stages.

### 1.4 Lessons from specific designs, read as process lessons rather than trivia

**ChaCha's schedule versus Salsa20's**: the shift from Salsa20's "column round then row round" to ChaCha's "column round then diagonal round" (companion ARX report `02` §1.2) is a rotation-*schedule*-adjacent topology decision more than a pure rotation-*constant* decision, but it illustrates the same process principle — Bernstein's own explicit framing of ChaCha as a diffusion-rate improvement over Salsa20 shows a designer returning to an already-published, already-trusted design specifically to re-run the diffusion-optimization step with a different topology, *without* needing to redo the entire architecture-choice and round-function-invention process from `01`–`02` — evidence that these process stages, once resolved, don't need to be revisited wholesale every time one specific decision (here, topology/schedule) is revisited.

**BLAKE2's G-function rotation constants**: inherited directly from ChaCha's quarter-round rather than independently re-searched, a genuine, explicit instance of a designer treating a prior design's already-validated rotation-constant search as reusable infrastructure rather than redoing the work — a real efficiency the process affords once a rotation schedule has survived enough independent scrutiny to be trusted as a component, not just as part of its original host cipher.

**Sparkle/Alzette's rotation-constant choices**: made *jointly* with the long-trail-strategy proof-methodology development (companion ARX report `03` §1.4, `05` §1.6's authorship overlap), illustrating that for a design whose primary goal is a *provable* bound rather than merely empirical margin, rotation-constant search and proof-methodology development are not sequential steps — the constants were chosen, in part, *because* they made the eventual per-ARX-box probability bound cleanly establishable, a genuinely different design pressure than pure diffusion-speed optimization alone would produce, and worth naming as a distinct pattern: **when provable security is the explicit goal, rotation-constant search is partly steered by "what will be provable," not only by "what diffuses fastest."**

**Known failures, as process lessons**: designs (real and hypothetical, per companion ARX report `08` §2.1, §3.3) that skip §1.3's automated-search escalation and rely solely on diffusion-speed/SAC-compliance testing to validate rotation-constant choices are the single most commonly-cited failure pattern in independent, non-institutionally-embedded design efforts — not because the designers were careless in some general sense, but because the process step they skipped (automated MILP/SAT/RX evaluation) is specifically the step that catches exactly the kind of residual structural weakness diffusion-metric testing is blind to (companion ARX report `03` §2.6, §2.8).

## 2. Performance Engineering as a Continuous, Not Terminal, Activity

### 2.1 The mistake of treating performance as a final measurement step

The single most important correction this section makes to how performance is usually taught: **performance engineering does not happen after the design is finished — it happens at every elimination pass in `02` §2.1, and it directly shapes which round-function and topology candidates survive to the next pass.** A designer who finishes a complete specification and *then* discovers it performs poorly has skipped every one of the cheap, early performance-estimation checks this report's reconstructed process relies on, and is now facing an expensive, late-stage redesign rather than an early, cheap course correction.

### 2.2 Instruction count, latency, throughput, and dependency chains as design-time, not implementation-time, considerations

Every one of these (covered at the implementation level in companion ARX report `07` §1) is *estimated*, not measured, during design — a designer sketches a candidate round function's instruction sequence and reasons, by hand or via a quick script, about how many independent operations exist per round (directly bearing on how well the candidate will parallelize under SIMD, per `02` §1.3's alignment discussion) and how long the longest *dependency chain* (operations that must happen strictly sequentially because each depends on the previous one's result) is — a round function with a long sequential dependency chain underutilizes any target CPU's out-of-order/multi-port execution capability regardless of its raw operation count, exactly the concern companion ARX report `02` §1.3 flags as a design-stage, not implementation-stage, decision. **The reconstructable design habit**: sketch the round function's operations as a small dependency-graph diagram (which operations can happen in parallel, which must wait on which) *before* writing any code, and use that diagram, not a finished benchmark, to compare candidates at the cheap-elimination-pass stage.

### 2.3 Carry chains and register pressure as joint constraints

Restating `02` §2.3's cryptanalytic carry-chain discussion from the performance angle: a design's carry-chain length choices affect not just nonlinearity budgeting but also, directly, how many intermediate values must remain live (occupying registers) at once during a round's computation — a round function whose operations can be reordered to minimize peak live-value count, without changing the mathematical operation sequence at all, is strictly better from a register-pressure standpoint at zero security cost, and this kind of reordering-for-register-pressure is a genuine, real design-time activity distinct from, and complementary to, the cryptanalytic carry-chain reasoning of `02` §2.3 — the two considerations (nonlinearity budgeting and register-pressure minimization) are evaluated together, not sequentially, precisely because both depend on the same underlying operation-ordering decisions.

### 2.4 Compiler optimization and SIMD awareness as design constraints, not implementation afterthoughts

Directly extending `02` §1.3's alignment discussion: a round function's operations should be sketched, from the earliest candidate stage, in a form a design-stage reviewer can already tell will auto-vectorize reasonably well (companion ARX report `07` §1.4) — independent, parallel operation groups exposed explicitly in the round function's own structure, not buried inside a single large, sequential-looking expression that a compiler's auto-vectorizer might or might not successfully untangle later. **The tradeoff with security, stated honestly**: there are documented cases across this report's case studies where a *slightly* less SIMD-friendly round-function structure would have offered marginally better diffusion-speed or rotational-symmetry properties, and the designer chose the more SIMD-friendly structure anyway, accepting a small, explicitly-acknowledged security-margin cost in exchange for a large, concretely-measured performance gain — ChaCha's column/diagonal structure is, again, the clearest documented instance, and the lesson worth taking from it is that **this tradeoff is made explicitly and with both sides measured, not resolved by default in either direction.**

## 3. Security Evaluation During Design — The Actual Evidentiary Ladder

### 3.1 What gets tested first, and why

Directly following `02` §2.1's elimination-pass structure, restated as a security-evaluation-specific ladder: **(1) structural/architectural fit** (does the candidate actually deliver on the chosen architecture's core promise, `01` §5) is checked first because it's nearly free and disqualifies candidates instantly; **(2) diffusion metrics** (SAC/BIC/avalanche, companion ARX report `03` §2) come next because they're cheap to compute empirically and, while insufficient as final evidence (companion ARX report `03` §2.6, `08` §2.1), they're an efficient early filter for candidates whose diffusion is simply too slow to be worth further, more expensive evaluation; **(3) rough, hand-derived or quick-script differential/linear characteristic estimation** comes next, cheaper than full automated search but sufficient to eliminate candidates with an obviously exploitable short trail; **(4) full MILP/SAT automated trail search** (companion ARX report `05`) is reserved for the small set of survivors, because building a correct model is genuinely expensive in researcher time; **(5) RX-cryptanalysis, differential-linear/PNB-style combined-attack evaluation, and — where relevant to the design's target — differential-neural evaluation** (companion ARX report `04` §2.3–2.4, §2.10) come last, applied to the one or two final candidates that have survived every earlier stage, precisely because this is the most expensive, most specialized tier of evaluation and running it against many candidates rather than the final few would be a poor use of research time.

### 3.2 What evidence convinces a designer to continue

Restating from inside the designer's decision process rather than the reviewer's (companion ARX report `08` §3.2 covers the reviewer's side in full): a designer continues investing in a candidate when each successive, more expensive evaluation stage in §3.1's ladder **fails to find anything alarming relative to the stated security-goal target from `01` §4**, and, critically, when the *margin* between the best attack the current evaluation stage finds and the design's target round count is comfortably wide, not just barely sufficient — a candidate that just barely survives its current evaluation stage is a candidate a designer should expect to lose more margin to the *next*, more sophisticated evaluation stage, not a candidate ready to move forward. The reconstructable pattern across every successful case study in `05`: published round counts consistently include explicit, stated margin beyond the best attack the design team's own evaluation found — Sparkle's published bounds and ChaCha's 20-round choice relative to the by-now 7.5-round best-known attack both reflect this margin-building instinct, applied at design time, not padded on afterward.

### 3.3 What evidence causes abandonment

Symmetrically: a candidate is abandoned when a mid-ladder evaluation stage (most commonly, per the historical record traced in companion history report `07` §1, full automated trail search) finds an attack whose complexity is uncomfortably close to the design's stated security-goal target, *and* no small, targeted structural change (an extra round, a different rotation constant, a different constant-injection schedule) closes the gap without triggering a costly redesign of an earlier-stage decision. **The distinction between "add a round" and "go back to `02`" matters as a real decision point**: a small parameter adjustment that resolves the finding, verified by re-running the relevant evaluation stage, is a normal, expected part of the iterative loop this section describes — a finding that can only be resolved by revisiting the round function's fundamental structure (going back to `02` §2.1's elimination process) or the architecture choice itself (`01` §5) is the point at which a genuinely experienced designer, per the design philosophies profiled in `06`, cuts losses on the specific candidate rather than continuing to patch it, because a design that has needed repeated fundamental restructuring to survive its own evaluation ladder is exactly the kind of design a reviewer will later distrust regardless of how it eventually performs (companion ARX report `08` §2.4's "asserting rather than establishing" mistake is the published-paper version of exactly this failure to cut losses early enough).

### 3.4 How designers know a design is "mature" — the actual, operational answer

Directly answering your original prompt's "how do designers know when a design is mature" question: maturity, reconstructed across every case study this report examines, is not a feeling — it is the **specific, checkable state of having cleared every rung of §3.1's evidentiary ladder with stated, comfortable margin, having a reference implementation that independently verifies the specification's own claims (companion ARX report `07` §1.6), and having exhausted the cheap-to-expensive evaluation escalation to the point where the *next* increment of scrutiny would require either external, independent cryptanalysis (which a single design team cannot manufacture for themselves) or an entirely new attack technique not yet invented (which, by definition, cannot be checked for in advance).** A design is "ready to publish," in the operational sense this report can actually specify, exactly when the design team has done everything *internally checkable* and the honest next step is exposing the design to the field's external, adversarial scrutiny — which is precisely why publication, not some private internal "done" feeling, is the field's actual maturity gate, and why `04`'s reviewer-expectations material is not a separate topic from this section but its direct continuation.
