# 04 — Reviewer Expectations and Design Mistakes, From the Designer's Side

*Part of a multi-file report — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map. Assumes `01`–`03`. Your companion ARX-specific report's `08` covers reviewer mindset and named mistakes in full technical depth from the reviewer's side; this file deliberately does not repeat that material — it reframes the same terrain as a set of things a designer should anticipate and preempt *during* design, not discover at submission time.*

## 1. Designing to Preempt, Not Just Satisfy, Review

### 1.1 The reframing that matters

Companion ARX report `08` §3.4 lists the concrete checklist a reviewer expects satisfied. The design-process reframing: **every item on that checklist should be something the design team already has evidence for by the time they sit down to write the paper, not something they scramble to produce in response to a reviewer's request.** This sounds obvious stated directly, but it inverts how many first-time designers actually work — the honest, reconstructable pattern across successful designs is that the evaluation ladder in `03` §3.1 *is* the checklist, run proactively during design, not a list of boxes to check off retroactively before submission. A design team that discovers, at write-up time, that they have no RX-cryptanalysis evidence has not "forgotten a section" — they have skipped an actual evaluation stage, and the honest fix is to go back and run it, not to write around its absence.

### 1.2 Anticipating the specific red flags, one at a time

Restating companion ARX report `08` §3.3's red-flag list, each reframed as a preemptable design-time decision:

- **"No MILP/SAT trail-search evidence at all"** is preempted by treating `03` §3.1's evaluation ladder as mandatory, not optional, for every serious candidate — this is not a reviewer's arbitrary demand; it's the same evidence a careful designer needs internally to know whether their own design is any good, which is exactly why `03` §3.4 defines maturity in terms of having cleared this ladder.
- **"Unsubstantiated N-bit security claims"** are preempted by never writing a security-level number into the specification that isn't traceable to a specific concrete-security derivation (companion ARX report `05` §3.4) — if you can't point to the calculation, don't write the number, and if the number came from "well, our state is N bits so it should be N-bit secure," that's not yet a derivation, per `03` §3.1's fourth and fifth evaluation rungs.
- **"Benchmark comparisons that don't disclose implementation-optimization-level parity"** are preempted by the discipline, established in `03` §2, of comparing your candidate against a *comparably-optimized* implementation of whatever you're benchmarking against — if you haven't invested equal optimization effort in the comparison baseline, say so explicitly in the writeup rather than let an unstated asymmetry stand.
- **"Missing engagement with recent literature"** is preempted, mechanically, by treating the reading roadmap in companion ARX report `10` (or the chronological equivalent in the companion history report `09`) as a living checklist to re-verify against shortly before submission, not a one-time reading assignment completed at the start of the project — a fast-moving sub-field (ChaCha's creeping attack record is the standing example) can genuinely move during the months a design project takes, and the field's reviewers, per companion ARX report `08` §3.3, notice this gap immediately because they are, by the nature of who reviews for these specific venues, closely tracking exactly this literature themselves.
- **"Claiming a provable bound without doing the actual bound-establishment work"** is preempted by the discipline `03` §1.4 names explicitly for Sparkle/Alzette specifically: if provable security is your goal, rotation-constant search and proof-methodology development must happen *together*, not with the proof asserted by analogy after the constants are already fixed.

### 1.3 What "publishable contribution" means, reframed for the designer who hasn't published before

Companion ARX report `08` §3.5's finding — that the field's actual publication currency is overwhelmingly "a new, independently-evaluable technique demonstrated on a design," not "a new design alone, self-evaluated" — has a direct, actionable design-process consequence worth stating for a first-time designer specifically: **decide, at the requirements-engineering stage (`01` §4), what your independently-defensible contribution beyond the design itself will be, and let that decision shape the design process alongside your security/performance goals, not as an afterthought bolted onto the writeup.** If your contribution is a new rotation-constant search method, `03` §1's process should be your paper's actual center of gravity, with the specific permutation you demonstrate it on being comparatively secondary — exactly the relationship Sparkle/Alzette's own paper has between its long-trail-strategy methodology and its specific Alzette ARX-box. If you don't yet know what your independent contribution is beyond "I designed a cipher," that's a sign, per `01` §3's novelty-evaluation discussion, that you should pause and identify one before investing further design effort — not because a design without one is worthless, but because it will face a genuinely harder path to serious institutional attention regardless of its technical merit.

## 2. Design Mistakes, Reframed as Process Failures Rather Than Outcome Failures

Companion ARX report `08` §2 catalogs specific named mistakes (confusing diffusion with security, weak benchmarks, inadequate threat models, ignoring prior art, misusing PractRand, misinterpreting avalanche) with historical grounding. This section's contribution is different: identifying **which specific process step, from `01`–`03`, each mistake is actually a symptom of skipping** — because knowing the mistake's name doesn't prevent it nearly as reliably as knowing which process step, done properly, would have caught it.

| Named mistake (companion ARX report `08` §2) | The process step that, done properly, prevents it |
|---|---|
| Confusing diffusion with security | `03` §3.1's evaluation ladder — treating diffusion metrics (rung 2) as sufficient evidence, rather than an early filter before rungs 3–5 |
| Weak benchmarks / strawman baselines | `03` §2's comparably-optimized-baseline discipline |
| Confirmation bias / improper statistical testing | `01` §2's falsifiable-hypothesis framing — a design tested against an unfalsifiable ambition has no principled stopping condition that would surface disconfirming evidence in the first place |
| Inadequate threat models | `01` §4's explicit threat-model requirement, stated and scoped (including what's *out* of scope) before any design work begins |
| Ignoring prior art / overclaiming novelty | `01` §3's novelty-evaluation discipline — checking whether your "new" round structure is equivalent, under the relabelings in companion ARX report `04` §1.3, to something already analyzed |
| Confusing randomness with unpredictability / misusing PractRand | `03` §3.1's evaluation-ladder ordering — treating statistical test-suite passage (a cheap, early-tier check) as if it were a late-tier, adversarial-cryptanalysis-grade result |
| Poor experimental controls | `03` §3.2–3.3's margin-tracking discipline — a candidate whose survival is barely-above-threshold at one evaluation stage, rather than comfortably above it, is exactly the kind of result poor controls would fail to distinguish from a genuinely secure candidate |
| Incomplete citations / weak reproducibility | §1.2 above's living-checklist discipline, and companion ARX report `08` §1.4's artifact-evaluation expectation |

**The meta-lesson this table is actually making**: every one of these named mistakes has the same underlying shape — *skipping or compressing a process stage that, done in full, would have surfaced the problem before it reached a reviewer.* This is genuinely good news for a designer trying to avoid them, because it means the mistakes aren't a list of arbitrary pitfalls to separately memorize and watch for — they're the predictable, recurring consequence of shortcutting the same evaluation ladder `03` already describes, and the single most effective defense against essentially all of them simultaneously is discipline about that ladder's ordering and rigor, not vigilance against each mistake individually.

### 2.1 A mistake this report can name that companion ARX report `08` doesn't emphasize as strongly: architecture-choice reversal

Directly extending `01` §5's closing point: a design where the architecture family (ARX vs. SPN vs. sponge) was evidently chosen *first*, for reasons of personal preference or familiarity, with the requirements from `01` §4 written or emphasized afterward to justify it, is a genuine, distinct failure mode this report's process-reconstruction approach is specifically positioned to name — because it's invisible in the *finished specification* (a specification doesn't announce "the architecture was chosen before the requirements") but visible the moment you try to reconstruct the design's actual decision sequence and find the requirements don't cleanly, independently point toward the chosen architecture the way `01` §5's decision procedure would predict. **How to check yourself for this specific mistake**: write out your requirements from `01` §4 fully, then hand them (without naming your chosen architecture) to someone else and ask what architecture family the requirements alone would suggest — if the answer doesn't match what you actually built, you may have reversed the order, and it's worth being honest with yourself about that before proceeding further, since a reviewer applying the same check, per companion ARX report `08` §3.3, will notice the mismatch even without access to your private design history.
