## 1. Where the idea actually comes from

Reconstructing across every case study this report and its companion ARX-specific document examine, the honest answer to "where do cryptographic design ideas originate" is almost never "a cryptographer sat down and imagined a new permutation." It is, overwhelmingly, **a specific, dated, external pressure that an existing primitive fails to handle acceptably**:

- Salsa20 exists because, in 2005, table-lookup AES leaked through cache timing and there was no good software-only fix at acceptable performance (companion history report, `02`).
- The sponge construction exists because Merkle–Damgård's length-extension weakness had been a known, nagging structural wart since 2002–2003, sharpened by MD5/SHA-1's collapse into an actual crisis (companion history report, `02`).
- Sparkle/Alzette exists because ARX designs, despite two decades of real-world trust, lacked AES's specific *kind* of assurance — a provable bound, not just an absence of found attacks (companion history report, `04`).
- Threefry/Philox exist because scientific/GPU computing needed a PRNG whose *N*th output is computable independently of the first *N*-1, a requirement no sequential stream-cipher-derived generator satisfies by construction (companion ARX report, `02` §2.5).
- Ascon exists because NIST's Lightweight Cryptography competition created an explicit, dated, external forcing function (a call for proposals) that made "someone should design for genuinely constrained devices" into a concrete, competition-scale opportunity.

**The pattern worth internalizing**: novelty for its own sake essentially never appears as the originating motivation in any well-regarded design this report or its companions examined. This matters practically, not just historically — if you find yourself starting from "wouldn't it be interesting to try a permutation with property X," you have skipped the step every successful design in the record actually started from, and you should go back and find the *external* pressure your design is meant to relieve before doing anything else. If you can't state that pressure in one sentence, you don't yet have a design brief; you have a fascination, and fascinations don't survive peer review on their own (`04`).

## 2. How the unstated need becomes a hypothesis

Once the pressure is identified, the next move — visible explicitly in Bernstein's own public design notes for Salsa20, and in the Sparkle/Alzette paper's framing of the long-trail strategy as its stated contribution — is to convert the pressure into a **falsifiable, scoped technical hypothesis**, not an ambition. "I want a fast, safe cipher" is an ambition. "A design built entirely from addition, rotation, and XOR can eliminate the cache-timing attack surface of table-lookup ciphers while matching or exceeding their throughput" is a hypothesis — it names the specific mechanism (no table lookups), the specific property being bought (constant-time-by-construction), and the specific cost being tested against (throughput parity or better). "SPN-style S-box nonlinearity can be replicated by an addition-based ARX-box with a *formally provable* bound, not just an empirically-tested one" is Sparkle/Alzette's hypothesis, similarly scoped and falsifiable — and, notably, the paper's actual contribution is exactly that hypothesis tested and confirmed, not merely "here is a new permutation."

**Why the falsifiability matters mechanically, not just philosophically**: a scoped hypothesis tells you, in advance, what evidence would make you abandon the direction — which is precisely the question Part `03`'s "how designers know when to stop" section depends on having an answer to. A designer who starts from an unfalsifiable ambition ("I want something elegant and secure") has no principled stopping condition and, per `04`'s common-mistakes catalog, tends to either iterate forever or stop arbitrarily whenever the design merely *feels* finished — neither of which the field's actual evaluation apparatus rewards.

## 3. Evaluating novelty — the field's actual, counterintuitive standard

Restating and grounding the Executive Summary's second correction: across every design philosophy this report profiles in `06`, novelty is evaluated **not by "is this idea new" but by "is this idea new in a place where newness earns its keep."** Sparkle/Alzette's actual novel contribution is a *proof methodology* (the long-trail strategy) — not, primarily, the specific 64-bit Alzette ARX-box, which is a comparatively modest, almost workmanlike arrangement of add/rotate/XOR/constant steps once you have the methodology that justifies it. Gohr's 2019 neural-cryptanalysis paper (companion history report, `04`) is novel in *applying* a known machine-learning technique to a well-studied cryptanalytic target, not in inventing new machine learning. The pattern: **the field rewards novelty concentrated at exactly one carefully-chosen point in a design's argument, surrounded by everything else being as conventional, well-understood, and independently-verifiable as possible.** A design that is novel *everywhere* — a new state layout, a new round function, new rotation constants, a new proof technique, all at once — is, per `04`'s reviewer-mindset material, not more impressive; it is *harder to review*, because a reviewer has no independently-trusted anchor point to reason from, and "we couldn't fully evaluate this" is functionally a rejection regardless of how the design actually performs.

## 4. Requirements engineering — the questions that must be answered before any pseudocode

Every case study this report examines shows the same requirements being pinned down, explicitly or implicitly, before the round function existed:

- **Security goal, stated as a specific number and a specific adversary model**: "128-bit security against a classical adversary with chosen-plaintext access," not "very secure." Sparkle explicitly targets stated bit-security levels per variant (Sparkle256/384/512); Ascon's submission documentation states its security claims per parameter set precisely enough that NIST's competition evaluation could compare candidates on a like-for-like basis.
- **Performance goal, stated relative to a named comparison point**: ChaCha's stated goal was explicitly *relative to Salsa20* (better diffusion per round, at least comparable speed), not an absolute number pulled from nowhere — a pattern worth copying: state your performance goal as "at least as fast as X, achieving property Y that X lacks," which gives you both a concrete target and a built-in significance argument for your eventual paper.
- **Threat model, including what's explicitly out of scope**: Salsa20/ChaCha's design brief is explicit that side-channel resistance is bought *by construction* against cache-timing specifically, not a general claim to resist every side-channel class (power analysis and fault attacks remain a live, separate implementation-level concern regardless of the ARX choice, per companion ARX report `07`) — stating what you are *not* claiming to solve is as load-bearing a requirements-engineering step as stating what you are.
- **Target platform(s), stated concretely**: Ascon and Sparkle's target platforms (resource-constrained IoT/embedded devices) are named specifically enough to justify every subsequent state-size and word-size decision (`02`); a design that claims to target "general use" without narrowing further tends, per `04`, to produce a specification that's mediocre everywhere rather than excellent somewhere.
- **Portability requirements, stated as which SIMD widths/architectures must remain viable**: ChaCha's four-way-parallel quarter-round structure is a direct consequence of a portability requirement (must vectorize well on commodity SIMD without requiring bespoke hardware) stated early enough to shape the round function's structure itself, not retrofitted after the fact (companion ARX report `02` §1.3).
- **Memory/footprint limits, stated in concrete units** where relevant: the entire NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition's evaluation criteria (companion ARX report `07` §3.2) forced every serious candidate — Ascon, Sparkle, Xoodyak, TinyJambu — to state gate-count and RAM/ROM-footprint targets explicitly, and the comparative table those targets produced is precisely what let NIST's committee make a defensible, evidence-based selection.
- **Auditability/implementation-simplicity requirements**: stated most explicitly in WireGuard's design philosophy (companion general-cryptography report, `05` §3.2) — a deliberate, named tradeoff of algorithm-agility flexibility for a smaller, more exhaustively-reviewable codebase — and echoed in every ARX design's preference for a small, uniform operation set precisely because uniformity aids independent review.
- **Future-proofing, stated honestly rather than promised**: no design this report examines claims permanent security; the honest version of future-proofing, visible in Sparkle's explicit round-count margin above its proven bound and in every NIST competition's explicit multi-round structure, is "state the margin you're building in, and state it as a number," not "trust that this will remain secure."

**The discipline point**: every one of these should be answerable, in writing, *before* the round function exists. A design that can't yet state its target bit-security level, its comparison-point performance goal, or its target platform has not yet finished requirements engineering — and per `04`, "we jumped straight to a round function because it felt exciting" is a real, named, recurring failure mode of independent (non-institutionally-embedded) design efforts specifically.

## 5. Choosing the architecture family — the decision that comes before any specific design

Once requirements are pinned down, the single highest-leverage decision a designer makes — before inventing anything — is which broad architecture family (ARX, SPN, Feistel, sponge/permutation, counter-based) the requirements actually point toward. This is a decision with real, hard tradeoffs, not a matter of taste, and the case studies in `05` show the reasoning explicitly enough to reconstruct a genuine decision procedure:

**Choose ARX when**: the target platform mix includes hardware without dedicated symmetric-cipher acceleration (mobile, embedded, IoT), constant-time-by-construction is valued highly enough to accept giving up S-box-based nonlinearity's cleaner theoretical bounds, and the design's primary deployment mode is software. This is Salsa20/ChaCha's, Speck's, and LEA's reasoning exactly.

**Choose (AND-)SPN or Simon-style AND-RX when**: hardware gate-count/footprint is the dominant constraint rather than general-purpose software throughput — AND gates are cheaper in silicon than either a full S-box table or a modular adder's carry-chain circuitry, which is precisely why Simon (hardware-optimized) was released as Speck's (software-optimized) explicit structural counterpart, a matched pair covering both regimes rather than one design trying to cover both.

**Choose a full designed S-box SPN when**: you have (or can build) the expertise to design and formally characterize a nonlinear component with near-optimal, jointly-tuned differential-uniformity and nonlinearity properties (AES's S-box, companion history report `01`) — a genuinely harder, more specialized undertaking than ARX's "derive nonlinearity from addition" shortcut, but one that, when done well, gives cleaner, more classically-provable wide-trail-strategy bounds.

**Choose sponge/permutation-based construction when**: you need a single primitive to flexibly serve multiple roles (hash, MAC, AEAD, and PRNG all from one permutation, per Xoodyak's explicit "Cyclist mode" design goal) or when Merkle–Damgård's length-extension weakness is specifically unacceptable for your use case, or when a strong, general-purpose *permutation* (rather than a specific mode) is your actual desired output because you expect the community to build multiple different modes on top of it.

**Choose counter-based (non-sponge, non-stream-cipher) design when**: your actual requirement is embarrassing parallelism and trivial seek-ahead (any output computable independently, in any order) rather than sequential-stream generation — Threefry/Philox's entire reason for existing, and a requirement no amount of clever engineering retrofits onto a fundamentally sequential stream-cipher design.

**The meta-lesson, stated directly**: this decision is made by matching the requirements list from §4 against these architecture families' known, structural tradeoffs — not by starting with a favorite architecture and writing requirements that happen to justify it backward. Every case study this report examines shows the requirements coming first; a design where the architecture was clearly chosen first and the requirements retrofitted is exactly the kind of thing an experienced reviewer (`04`) notices immediately, because the tradeoffs described in this section stop lining up cleanly once the ordering is reversed.
