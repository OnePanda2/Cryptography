## 1. The Primitive Zoo — Executive Summary

Before diving into symmetric cryptography specifically, it's worth having the full map of *primitive types* in view, because a huge amount of practitioner error comes from reaching for the wrong primitive category (using encryption where a MAC or commitment was needed; using a plain hash where a KDF was needed) rather than a flawed instance of the right one.

| Primitive | What it guarantees | What it does *not* guarantee | Canonical construction(s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetric (block/stream) encryption | Confidentiality, given a shared key | Integrity/authenticity (on its own) | AES, ChaCha20 |
| Hash function | Collision/preimage resistance, determinism | Confidentiality, keying | SHA-256, SHA3-256, BLAKE3 |
| MAC | Integrity + authenticity, given a shared key | Confidentiality, non-repudiation | HMAC-SHA256, Poly1305, CMAC |
| Authenticated Encryption (AEAD) | Confidentiality + integrity + authenticity together | Non-repudiation | AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305, AES-GCM-SIV |
| Digital signature | Authenticity + integrity + non-repudiation, public verifiability | Confidentiality | RSA-PSS, ECDSA, Ed25519, ML-DSA |
| Key exchange | Shared secret derivation over a public channel | Authentication (on its own — needs signatures/PKI layered on) | Diffie–Hellman, ECDH, ML-KEM |
| Commitment scheme | Hiding (value stays secret until opened) + binding (can't change your mind) | — | Hash-based commitments, Pedersen commitments |
| Secret sharing | Distributes a secret so only a threshold of shares reconstructs it | Confidentiality of a *shared* value against fewer than threshold | Shamir's scheme, Blakley's scheme |
| Zero-knowledge proof | Prove a statement true without revealing why | — | Schnorr protocol (interactive), zk-SNARKs/STARKs (non-interactive) |
| Oblivious transfer (OT) | Sender sends one of several messages; sender doesn't learn which was chosen, receiver learns only that one | — | Foundational building block for MPC |
| Multi-party computation (MPC) | Joint computation over private inputs, revealing only the output | — | Garbled circuits (Yao), secret-sharing-based (GMW, SPDZ family) |
| Homomorphic encryption | Computation directly on ciphertexts | Efficiency (still costly, esp. for FHE) | Paillier (additive/partial), CKKS/BFV/TFHE (fully homomorphic) |
| Threshold cryptography | Splits a cryptographic *operation* (decryption, signing) across parties, no party alone can perform it | — | Threshold ECDSA/Schnorr, threshold FHE |
| Verifiable Delay Function (VDF) | Proof that a specific amount of *sequential* (non-parallelizable) time elapsed | — | Wesolowski, Pietrzak constructions |
| Verifiable Random Function (VRF) | Deterministic, publicly-verifiable pseudorandom output tied to a secret key | — | RSA-FDH-based, EC-based (used in blockchain leader election) |
| Accumulator | Compactly represents a (large, dynamic) set with efficient membership/non-membership proofs | — | RSA accumulators, Merkle-tree-based accumulators |
| Identity-based encryption (IBE) | Public key derivable directly from an identity string (email, etc.), no separate certificate needed | — | Boneh–Franklin (pairing-based) |

The rest of Part 2 focuses on **symmetric** primitives (encryption, hashing, MACs, modes). Part 3 covers public-key primitives in depth. Part 6 covers the more exotic entries in this table (ZK, MPC, FHE, threshold, VDF/VRF, accumulators) as part of the research frontier.

## 2. Block Ciphers

### 2.1 Structural paradigms

**Feistel networks**: split the block into halves (L, R); each round computes L' = R, R' = L ⊕ F(R, K_i) for a round function F (which need not be invertible — this is the whole point; Feistel structure guarantees invertibility of the overall cipher regardless of F's own invertibility, because decryption just runs the same steps in reverse). Proven (by **Luby and Rackoff**, 1988) that 3 rounds of Feistel with independent pseudorandom round functions yield a pseudorandom *permutation* from pseudorandom *functions* — a foundational construction-from-primitive result, not just an engineering trick. DES and its many descendants (3DES, Blowfish, Twofish's core, Camellia) are Feistel networks.

**Substitution-Permutation Networks (SPN)**: apply a layer of parallel, small nonlinear substitutions (S-boxes, delivering Shannon's *confusion*) followed by a linear mixing/permutation layer (delivering *diffusion*), repeated for several rounds, with round keys XORed in between (key whitening). AES, Serpent, and most modern designs are SPNs — they parallelize better in hardware/SIMD than Feistel networks and are easier to formally analyze for differential/linear resistance because the S-box and diffusion layers can be characterized independently.

**ARX (Add-Rotate-XOR)**: build the cipher entirely from modular addition, bit rotation, and XOR — no S-box lookup table at all. This gives constant-time execution "for free" on essentially any CPU (no table lookups means no cache-timing side channel of the classic AES-software-implementation kind), which is precisely why Bernstein designed Salsa20/ChaCha20 this way, and it's a major reason ChaCha20 became the preferred stream cipher for software-only, side-channel-conscious contexts (mobile, embedded, and eventually TLS via RFC 7905/8439).

### 2.2 The historical arc: DES → 3DES → AES

- **DES** (1977, FIPS 46): 64-bit block, 56-bit key, 16-round Feistel. Its S-boxes were, decades later, shown (by Coppersmith, an original IBM designer, publishing in 1994 once cleared to do so) to have been deliberately hardened against **differential cryptanalysis**, a technique the NSA evidently knew about in the 1970s but which the open research community only rediscovered and published in 1990 (Biham and Shamir). This is one of the field's most-cited illustrations of the gap that can exist between classified and open cryptanalytic knowledge, and of Kerckhoffs's-principle-compatible design surviving scrutiny it wasn't obviously built to survive at time of *disclosure*. DES's fatal flaw was never cryptanalytic — it was simply too short a key for the compute available by the 1990s; brute force alone (the EFF's Deep Crack, 1998, ~56 hours) killed it operationally.
- **3DES** (Triple DES, ANSI X9.52): encrypt-decrypt-encrypt with (usually) two or three independent DES keys, giving an effective security level around 112 bits (not 168, due to a meet-in-the-middle attack that halves the naive doubling from using two keys — this is itself a classic illustration of why naive cascading doesn't multiply security the way one might hope). Still lingers in legacy payment systems (its 64-bit block size makes it vulnerable to birthday-bound collision attacks on large data volumes under CBC/CTR-like modes — the **Sweet32** attack, 2016) and is formally deprecated by NIST for new use.
- **AES** (Rijndael, 2001, FIPS 197): 128-bit block, 128/192/256-bit key, SPN with 10/12/14 rounds respectively. The S-box is the multiplicative inverse over GF(2^8) (mapping 0 → 0 by convention) composed with an affine transformation over GF(2), chosen because inversion has near-optimal nonlinearity and differential uniformity simultaneously — deliberately resisting both differential and linear cryptanalysis by explicit algebraic design, not by trial and error. The diffusion layer (ShiftRows + MixColumns, the latter a matrix multiplication in GF(2^8)) achieves full diffusion (every output byte of a full round depends on every input byte) in exactly 2 rounds, a property formally analyzed via the "wide trail strategy" that Daemen and Rijmen developed specifically to give provable bounds on the best possible differential/linear characteristic probability as a function of round count — this is *why* AES is trusted at the level it is: not merely "nobody has broken it," but "there's a proof that certain attack classes cannot do better than X, for this many rounds," which is a categorically stronger form of assurance than most historical ciphers ever had. **AES-NI** hardware instructions (introduced by Intel in 2010, now near-universal) additionally solved AES's historical cache-timing side-channel weakness (table-lookup-based software AES implementations leaked key bits via cache access patterns — a real, practically demonstrated attack class through the mid-2000s) by moving the S-box computation into fixed-latency silicon.

### 2.3 The AES finalists and other significant block ciphers

The AES competition (15 initial submissions, 5 finalists) is worth knowing beyond just the winner, because the finalists remain used, studied, and instructive as design-tradeoff case studies:
- **Serpent** (Anderson, Biham, Knudsen): extremely conservative design (32 rounds, deliberately more margin than deemed strictly necessary), an SPN with a bitslice-friendly S-box layer. Widely regarded as having the largest security margin of any finalist; slower than Rijndael in the hardware/software profile that mattered most to NIST's selection criteria at the time, which is a real illustration of security-margin-vs-performance tradeoffs actually driving a standardization outcome.
- **Twofish** (Schneier, Kelsey, Whiting, Wagner, Hall, Ferguson): Feistel-based with key-dependent S-boxes, a flexible and fast design; still used in some FDE (full-disk-encryption) software (e.g., some VeraCrypt cascades).
- **RC6** (Rivest et al.): derived from RC5, used data-dependent rotations; encumbered somewhat by patent concerns at the time, a real (non-cryptographic) factor in adoption outcomes worth noting as part of "why some survived and others didn't."
- **MARS** (IBM): a heterogeneous design mixing multiple round types; considered by some reviewers overly complex relative to its security margin.
- **Blowfish** (Schneier, 1993, pre-AES): 64-bit block Feistel cipher, still found in legacy systems; shares 3DES's birthday-bound exposure at large data volumes due to its 64-bit block.
- **Camellia** (NTT/Mitsubishi, 2000): a Feistel design comparable in security to AES, standardized by ISO/IEC, CRYPTREC (Japan), and NESSIE (EU); used in some TLS ciphersuites and Japanese government systems, but never displaced AES as the global default.
- **Ascon**: an ARX/sponge-hybrid *lightweight* cipher family (originally a CAESAR competition finalist, 2019), selected by NIST in **February 2023** as the winner of the Lightweight Cryptography standardization project, published as **SP 800-232** (2025) for resource-constrained devices (IoT, embedded) where full AES is too costly in power/silicon area. This is the newest "winner" in the symmetric-cipher standardization lineage and matters as the field's answer to "what do you use when you literally cannot afford AES."

### 2.4 Why some designs survived and others failed

Patterns worth internalizing, because they recur across the whole field, not just block ciphers:
- **Provable structural bounds beat "nobody's broken it yet."** AES's wide-trail-strategy bounds on differential/linear characteristic probability are why its trust is qualitatively different from, say, a cipher that's merely survived 20 years of attempted cryptanalysis without such a proof.
- **Simplicity aids analysis; analyzability is itself a security property.** MARS's relative complexity, versus Rijndael's clean, uniform round structure, is frequently cited as part of why Rijndael inspired more confidence for equal or better measured security — a design that's easier to reason about attracts more (and better) cryptanalysis, which is a *feature*, not a cost, over the long run.
- **Hardware/software performance profile matters as a real selection criterion**, not an afterthought — Serpent's conservatism cost it the AES title on performance grounds even though many reviewers considered its margin the best of the finalists.
- **Non-cryptographic factors (patents, licensing, government trust politics) shape real-world survival** independent of cryptanalytic quality — RC6's patent situation, Camellia's more limited geographic adoption despite AES-comparable security, and the historical trust deficit around NSA-influenced designs (fair or not) all illustrate this.

## 3. Stream Ciphers

Stream ciphers generate a pseudorandom **keystream** and combine it with plaintext (typically via XOR), rather than processing fixed-size blocks. Advantages: no padding needed, naturally handles arbitrary-length data, can be very fast in software. The central danger, structurally, is **keystream/nonce reuse**: reusing the same (key, nonce) pair produces the same keystream twice, and XORing the two resulting ciphertexts cancels the keystream entirely, leaking the XOR of the two plaintexts (the two-time-pad problem from Part 1, reincarnated at internet scale) — this single failure mode (nonce reuse, whether from a buggy counter, VM/container snapshot/restore, or insufficient nonce space) is one of the most common real-world cryptographic vulnerabilities across the entire industry, independent of which stream cipher is used.

- **RC4** (Rivest, 1987, reverse-engineered and published 1994): extremely simple, once ubiquitous (WEP, early TLS, WPA-TKIP). Suffers *statistical biases* in its keystream (some early-output bytes are measurably non-uniform — the Fluhrer–Mantin–Shamir and later "RC4 NOMORE" work exploited these), making it fully broken as a serious cryptographic primitive; formally prohibited in TLS (RFC 7465, 2015) and deprecated everywhere. A textbook case of a design that looked fine for over a decade until cryptanalysis caught up with real exploitability, and a big part of why the field is now much more conservative about trusting a cipher just because it hasn't yet been broken.
- **Salsa20 and ChaCha20** (Bernstein, 2005/2008): ARX-based, built around a pseudorandom permutation applied to a counter+nonce+key block, run in counter mode. ChaCha20 improves Salsa20's diffusion per round (better avalanche properties for the same round count) and is the version that won broad real-world adoption, primarily via **ChaCha20-Poly1305** (RFC 7539/8439) as the software-friendly, side-channel-resistant-by-construction alternative to AES-GCM, especially valuable on platforms without AES-NI hardware acceleration (mobile chipsets historically, some embedded targets) where AES-GCM in software is both slow *and* prone to timing leakage. Used by default in TLS 1.3, WireGuard, and Signal, among many others.

## 4. Hash Functions

### 4.1 The MD/SHA lineage and its collapse

- **MD5** (Rivest, 1992): 128-bit output, Merkle–Damgård. Practical collisions demonstrated by **Wang, Feng, Lai, and Yu** in 2004; by 2008 researchers (Sotirov et al.) used MD5 collisions to forge a rogue CA certificate, a real-world PKI-breaking demonstration. MD5 is fully broken for any security-relevant use and should never appear in new designs (non-cryptographic checksums are the only defensible remaining use).
- **SHA-1** (NSA-designed, NIST standardized, 1995): 160-bit output, same Merkle–Damgård/Davies–Meyer family. Theoretical collision weaknesses were published from 2005 onward (Wang et al. again), but a full practical collision — two distinct PDF files with identical SHA-1 hashes — wasn't publicly demonstrated until **Google and CWI Amsterdam's "SHAttered" attack (2017)**, using an estimated 2^63.1 SHA-1 computations. SHA-1 is formally deprecated (NIST disallowed it for digital signatures by end of 2030 in earlier guidance, and browsers/CAs stopped trusting SHA-1 certificates years before that) but remains present in some legacy git-object-hashing and other non-adversarial contexts where collision resistance isn't the security-critical property in play — a useful distinction for practitioners: *broken for collision resistance* is not automatically *broken for every conceivable use*, though new designs should never rely on that distinction.
- **SHA-2 family** (SHA-224/256/384/512/512-256, FIPS 180-4): still Merkle–Damgård/Davies–Meyer, but with a substantially larger, more analyzed compression function than SHA-1's. Despite sharing SHA-1's structural family, SHA-2 has withstood two decades of intense cryptanalysis with no meaningful practical weakness found — best published attacks reach reduced-round variants far short of the full 64/80 rounds used in SHA-256/512. **SHA-256 remains the single most widely deployed cryptographic hash function in the world** (Bitcoin proof-of-work, TLS certificate signatures, countless HMAC and KDF instantiations) as of 2026.

### 4.2 The sponge era: SHA-3/Keccak

NIST ran an open **SHA-3 competition** (2007–2012, mirroring the AES process) explicitly because the SHA-1/MD5 collapse raised legitimate concern that the entire Merkle–Damgård/Davies–Meyer *family* might share a structural weakness, and a structurally *different* backup was judged prudent even though SHA-2 itself was not (and still is not) broken — a deliberate, publicly-reasoned hedge, not a reaction to an actual SHA-2 break. **Keccak** (Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters, Van Assche) won, standardized as **FIPS 202 (2015)**.

**Sponge construction**: maintain a large internal state (1600 bits for Keccak-f[1600], split into a "rate" r and "capacity" c portion); *absorb* input by XORing message blocks into the rate portion and applying a fixed permutation between blocks; *squeeze* output by reading off the rate portion and applying the permutation again, as many times as needed for the desired output length. Security (against generic attacks) is governed by the capacity c: roughly min(c/2, output-length) bits of security against collisions. Sponges naturally:
- Resist **length-extension** (SHA-2's known weakness — see Part 1, 2.9): the internal state after absorbing a message isn't simply "the output," so an attacker who only knows H(m) cannot extend it, unlike Merkle–Damgård.
- Support **arbitrary-length, on-the-fly output** — the basis of **SHAKE128/SHAKE256**, extendable-output functions (XOFs) used pervasively inside newer constructions, including as a component of ML-KEM and ML-DSA (both use SHAKE internally for expansion/derivation).
- Generalize cleanly to the **duplex construction** (interleaved absorb/squeeze in a single pass), enabling efficient authenticated-encryption modes built directly from the permutation without a separate cipher+MAC composition — this is exactly the design pattern Ascon uses (a lightweight sponge/duplex-based AEAD, not just a hash), and it's a meaningful conceptual unification: "hash function" and "AEAD scheme" can be two API surfaces on the same underlying permutation-based primitive.

### 4.3 BLAKE / BLAKE2 / BLAKE3

**BLAKE** (Aumasson, Henzen, Meier, Phan) was a SHA-3 finalist that lost to Keccak but remained actively developed. **BLAKE2** (2012) is a from-scratch-optimized, faster-than-MD5-in-software hash with SHA-3-comparable security margins, widely adopted where raw software speed matters (used in libsodium, WireGuard's original design considerations, many checksumming tools, and as an option in Argon2's construction). **BLAKE3** (2020, Aumasson, O'Connor, Neves, Winnerlein) restructures the design around a Merkle-tree internal structure explicitly for **massive parallelism** (multi-core and SIMD), making it dramatically faster still on modern hardware while retaining a 128-bit security target; increasingly the pragmatic choice in new systems software where raw hashing throughput (content-addressed storage, build systems, checksumming large files) matters more than conservative multi-decade standardization pedigree.

### 4.4 Ascon as a hash function

Beyond its AEAD role (4.2 above and Part 2.5 below), the Ascon family (NIST SP 800-232, 2025) also specifies **Ascon-Hash256** and **Ascon-XOF128**, giving the lightweight-cryptography use case a matched hash function built on the same 320-bit sponge permutation as the cipher, so an embedded implementation can share code/hardware between hashing and AEAD needs — a practical, not just cryptanalytic, design win for constrained devices.

## 5. MACs, Authenticated Encryption, and Modes of Operation

### 5.1 MACs (Message Authentication Codes)

A MAC takes a shared key and a message and produces a short tag; verification recomputes and compares. Security notion: existential unforgeability under chosen-message attack (EUF-CMA) — an adversary who sees many (message, tag) pairs of their choosing still cannot produce a valid tag for a *new* message.

- **HMAC** (Bellare, Canetti, Krawczyk, 1996; RFC 2104; FIPS 198): HMAC_K(m) = H((K' ⊕ opad) ‖ H((K' ⊕ ipad) ‖ m)) for a hash function H — the nested construction exists specifically to neutralize length-extension (see 4.1/1.2.9), and HMAC has a clean security proof reducing to properties of the compression function, largely independent of full collision resistance of H (part of why HMAC-MD5 and HMAC-SHA1, while no longer recommended for new designs, were not broken by the same attacks that broke MD5/SHA-1 as plain hash functions — a genuinely important nuance often lost in casual "MD5 is broken, therefore never use it for anything" framing, though the conservative and now-universal practice is simply not to use deprecated hash functions in *any* new capacity).
- **CMAC/CBC-MAC family** (built from a block cipher rather than a hash): CMAC (NIST SP 800-38B) fixes real structural weaknesses in naive CBC-MAC (which is only secure for fixed-length messages unless carefully modified).
- **Poly1305** (Bernstein, 2005): a fast, information-theoretically-motivated one-time authenticator based on polynomial evaluation in GF(2^130−5), always paired with a per-message key derived from a stream cipher (ChaCha20 in the standard combination) — the "Poly1305" half of ChaCha20-Poly1305. Notably *not* a general-purpose keyed MAC on its own (the "one-time" in its design lineage matters); the combination with ChaCha20's keystream-derived one-time key is what makes the overall AEAD construction reusable across many messages under one long-term key.

### 5.2 Authenticated Encryption (AE/AEAD)

Plain encryption gives confidentiality but not integrity — an active attacker can flip ciphertext bits and, depending on mode, cause predictable or exploitable plaintext changes (padding-oracle attacks against CBC-mode-without-a-MAC being the classic, repeatedly-rediscovered example, from the original Vaudenay padding-oracle work through years of TLS-specific variants like Lucky13 and POODLE). The field's converged answer is **AEAD**: encrypt-and-authenticate as a single, misuse-resistant-where-possible primitive that also supports **associated data** (AD) — data that's authenticated but not encrypted (protocol headers, sequence numbers), critical for binding ciphertext to its context and defeating cut-and-paste/replay attacks across message boundaries.

- **Generic composition** (Encrypt-then-MAC, MAC-then-Encrypt, Encrypt-and-MAC): **Bellare and Namprempre** (2000) proved that of these three orderings, only **Encrypt-then-MAC** is generically secure for *any* choice of secure encryption scheme and secure MAC — MAC-then-Encrypt and Encrypt-and-MAC can each be insecure for specific (individually-secure) component choices, which is exactly the class of bug that produced years of real-world TLS CBC-mode padding-oracle vulnerabilities (TLS historically used MAC-then-Encrypt for its CBC ciphersuites). This is one of the cleanest, most consequential "generic composition is subtle, get the order right" lessons in applied cryptography.
- **AES-GCM** (Galois/Counter Mode, McGrew & Viega, NIST SP 800-38D): CTR-mode encryption combined with a GHASH polynomial-evaluation MAC over GF(2^128). Extremely fast with hardware AES-NI + carry-less multiplication (PCLMULQDQ) support, the dominant AEAD in TLS 1.2/1.3 and IPsec. Its critical, frequently-relearned failure mode: **nonce reuse is catastrophic**, not just for confidentiality (as with any CTR-family cipher) but for *authenticity* — reusing a nonce under GCM leaks the GHASH authentication key, after which an attacker can forge arbitrary authenticated ciphertexts, not merely recover plaintext. This is why GCM is specified with strict nonce-uniqueness requirements and why random 96-bit nonces are considered risky at very high message volumes under a single key (birthday-bound collision risk again) — motivating the misuse-resistant alternatives below.
- **ChaCha20-Poly1305** (RFC 8439): the ARX/software-friendly AEAD alternative to AES-GCM, standard in TLS 1.3, used heavily where AES-NI isn't available or where constant-time-by-construction is valued without needing hardware support.
- **AES-GCM-SIV** (Synthetic IV, RFC 8452, Gueron/Langley/Lindell): a **nonce-misuse-resistant** AEAD — if a nonce is accidentally reused, the *only* leakage is whether the same (key, nonce, plaintext, AD) tuple was used before (i.e., whether two ciphertexts are identical), not the catastrophic key/authenticity compromise GCM suffers. Achieved by deriving the actual IV/tag synthetically from a keyed PRF over the plaintext and AD, rather than trusting an externally-supplied nonce directly. A direct engineering response to how common nonce-management bugs turned out to be in practice — trading a small amount of performance and a two-pass requirement (must see the whole message before encrypting, unlike streaming GCM) for a dramatically safer failure mode.
- **OCB (Offset Codebook Mode)**: a single-pass AEAD mode offering both high performance and full misuse-resistance-adjacent properties from a different construction family (based on a block-cipher-based "offset" tweak scheme rather than SIV's PRF-derived IV); historically limited in adoption partly due to patent encumbrance for years (now expired/licensed more permissively), an again-recurring example of IP considerations shaping real deployment independent of cryptographic merit.
- **SIV mode generally** (RFC 5297, deterministic authenticated encryption): useful specifically where nonces genuinely cannot be guaranteed unique (key-wrapping applications, deterministic encryption for searchable/indexed encrypted databases where determinism is a *feature*), at the acknowledged cost of leaking plaintext equality between ciphertexts under the same key.
- **XTS mode** (IEEE P1619, NIST SP 800-38E): specifically designed for **disk/block-storage encryption**, where ciphertext must be exactly the same length as plaintext (no room for a MAC tag or nonce per sector) and random access to fixed-size sectors is required. XTS provides confidentiality and some resistance to block-level manipulation via a tweak (typically the sector number) but explicitly does **not** provide full AEAD-grade authenticity — a deliberate, documented tradeoff specific to the storage use case, not a general-purpose recommendation, and a common point of confusion for practitioners who assume "used by BitLocker/LUKS/FileVault" implies general-purpose AEAD-equivalent security.

### 5.3 Modes of operation not otherwise covered

- **ECB (Electronic Codebook)**: encrypts each block independently — **never use this**; identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks, leaking structural information (the famously-cited "ECB penguin" image, where encrypting a bitmap in ECB mode leaves the outline visible in the ciphertext, is the standard illustration used in essentially every applied-cryptography course).
- **CBC (Cipher Block Chaining)**: XORs each plaintext block with the previous ciphertext block before encrypting; requires an unpredictable IV, requires padding (historically the source of padding-oracle attacks when not combined with a MAC per 5.2 above), and is inherently sequential (cannot be parallelized for encryption, though decryption can be).
- **CTR (Counter Mode)**: turns a block cipher into a stream cipher by encrypting a counter (often nonce ‖ counter) and XORing with plaintext; fully parallelizable, no padding needed, but provides *no* integrity on its own (hence its near-universal pairing with a MAC, as in GCM = CTR + GHASH).

### 5.4 Performance and current recommendations (2026 baseline)

For new general-purpose designs: **AES-256-GCM** (with hardware acceleration) or **ChaCha20-Poly1305** (software-only/side-channel-conscious contexts) remain the pragmatic default AEAD choices; **AES-GCM-SIV** where nonce-uniqueness cannot be guaranteed with confidence; **Ascon-AEAD128** for constrained/lightweight devices per NIST SP 800-232; **SHA-256/SHA-512** or **SHA3-256/512** for general hashing, **BLAKE3** where raw throughput dominates and the (younger, less multi-decade-scrutinized) design is an acceptable tradeoff; **HMAC** or a KMAC (Keccak-based MAC, NIST SP 800-185) for MAC needs; and, per Part 3, a **hybrid classical+PQC** key-establishment scheme for anything with a long confidentiality horizon.
