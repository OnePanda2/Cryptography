## 1. Cryptanalysis

Cryptanalysis is the study of extracting information a cipher is supposed to hide — recovering keys, plaintexts, or distinguishing ciphertext from random with better-than-chance advantage. Every technique below answers three questions: **what assumption does the attack require** (known plaintext? chosen plaintext? adaptive access? physical proximity?), **what does it actually break** (full key recovery? partial key bits? a distinguisher only?), and **what does it not break** (does defeating it require a full redesign, or just more rounds/better parameters?).

### 1.1 Differential cryptanalysis

**Biham and Shamir (1990)**, though — as noted in Part 2 — IBM/NSA appear to have known the technique in the 1970s and hardened DES's S-boxes against it before public discovery. Core idea: track how a fixed input **difference** (ΔP, typically XOR difference) propagates through the cipher's rounds, with certain output differences occurring with probability higher than 2^(−n) for an n-bit block, due to the nonlinear S-box's imperfect diffusion. Chain high-probability **differential characteristics** across rounds; if the aggregate probability over R rounds is significantly above random, an attacker with enough chosen-plaintext pairs sharing the input difference can statistically distinguish the cipher from random, and often recover key bits from the final round via a partial-decryption/counting technique.

**Requires**: chosen plaintexts (in the strong form) — a real practical constraint that limits differential cryptanalysis's threat model in many deployed contexts, but not in protocol designs that let an adversary influence plaintext (e.g., some padding-oracle-adjacent scenarios, or cryptographic hash functions used in contexts where the "plaintext" is attacker-controlled input).

**What breaks / what doesn't**: full DES (16 rounds) needs about 2^47 chosen plaintexts under the best classical differential attack — worse than exhaustive key search (2^56) for the standard configuration, meaning differential cryptanalysis alone never became the *practical* break of full DES (brute force was), though it was devastating against reduced-round DES variants and directly shaped how many rounds later ciphers needed. AES's wide-trail-strategy design gives *provable* bounds on the best possible differential characteristic probability as a function of round count specifically to make differential cryptanalysis provably infeasible for the full cipher, not merely empirically untested — the strongest form of assurance the field currently knows how to give against this attack class.

### 1.2 Linear cryptanalysis

**Mitsuru Matsui (1993)**: find a **linear approximation** — a linear combination of plaintext bits, ciphertext bits, and key bits that holds with probability significantly different from 1/2 (the "linear bias") — over the full cipher, built by chaining linear approximations of individual S-boxes/rounds (Matsui's "piling-up lemma" gives the approximate combined bias as a function of individual round biases). Given enough **known plaintexts** (not chosen — a strictly weaker, more realistic requirement than differential cryptanalysis, part of why linear cryptanalysis is often considered the more practically dangerous of the two classical statistical attacks), an attacker can recover key bits via a maximum-likelihood counting procedure. Matsui's own attack broke full DES using 2^43 known plaintexts, faster than exhaustive search and a genuine, practical, first-of-its-kind full break of a real deployed standard cipher.

**What breaks / doesn't**: AES's S-box was chosen for near-optimal nonlinearity specifically to minimize any single S-box's linear bias, and the wide-trail strategy again gives provable multi-round bounds; no linear attack comes remotely close to threatening full AES.

### 1.3 Higher-order and structural statistical attacks

- **Integral / Square attacks** (originally developed to attack the Square cipher, a Rijndael/AES precursor by the same designers, and later adapted as a core AES cryptanalysis tool): track how a *set* of plaintexts, chosen so that one input byte takes all 256 values while others are fixed, propagates — after enough rounds, certain output bytes' XOR-sum over the whole set becomes predictable (zero, in the simplest case) with probability 1, a purely structural (not statistical/probabilistic) property, at least for a limited number of rounds. This is the strongest currently-known structural distinguisher against reduced-round AES and directly informed why AES uses the specific number of rounds it does (integral attacks reach further than differential/linear attacks against reduced-round AES, and the full round count includes margin against integral-attack extensions too).
- **Boomerang attacks** (Wagner, 1999): chain two *short* differential characteristics — one for the first half of the cipher, one for the second half, each individually higher-probability than a single characteristic spanning the whole cipher would be — connected via a clever "boomerang" quartet-of-plaintexts structure. Effective against ciphers where a single strong differential doesn't span enough rounds but two shorter ones from each half do; **amplified/rectangle boomerang** variants extend the technique further and have been the basis of some of the strongest published attacks against reduced-round versions of ciphers like Serpent and KASUMI (the 3G/4G mobile cipher, practically broken in this way for related-key settings, informing why unrelated-key security and boomerang resistance are now explicit design goals).
- **Impossible differential cryptanalysis**: rather than chasing a *high-probability* differential, find a differential with **probability exactly zero** (an "impossible" propagation) over some number of rounds, then use it as a sieve — any key candidate for which the impossible differential *would* occur is provably wrong and can be discarded (a "miss in the middle" technique, meeting two half-cipher impossible propagations in the center). Has produced some of the best known attacks against reduced-round AES and several other SPN ciphers.
- **Meet-in-the-middle (MITM) attacks**: exploit a cipher's decomposability into two (or more) independently-computable stages by computing forward from the plaintext side and backward from the ciphertext side separately, then matching in the middle — the classic technique that shows 2-key triple-DES only achieves ~2^(2·56)/2 = effectively far less than naive 112-bit security (the reason 3DES's real security is ~112 bits, not 168, as noted in Part 2). Modern MITM variants (with "splice-and-cut," bicliques, and other refinements) are the technique family behind the fastest published attacks on full-round AES itself, and were the specific technique class improved 200-800x by Anthropic's 2026 AI-cryptanalysis result against reduced-round (7 of 10) AES-128 — an important detail: even that headline-making result was an *improvement to an existing, well-understood MITM attack technique*, not a fundamentally new attack class, and it targeted an academically-weakened cipher variant, not full AES.
- **Biclique cryptanalysis** (Bogdanov, Khovratovich, Rechberger, 2011): the technique behind the best published *full-round* AES key-recovery attacks (marginally faster than exhaustive search — roughly a factor of 4 for AES-128, i.e., about 2^126 instead of 2^128 operations) — a genuinely real, published, peer-reviewed result, and simultaneously a perfect illustration of "statistically significant but operationally meaningless": no computer in existence or foreseeable can execute 2^126 operations, so full AES remains completely secure in every practical sense despite this technically-valid academic break existing. This distinction (marginal-but-real theoretical break vs. practical exploitability) is one of the most important things for a newcomer to internalize about how the field actually talks about "broken."
- **Cube attacks** (Dinur, Shamir, 2009): treat a cipher's output bit as a multivariate polynomial (over GF(2)) in the key and plaintext/IV bits, and exploit the algebraic structure of low-degree "superpolys" derived by summing over structured subsets ("cubes") of public variables — most effective against stream ciphers and lightweight primitives with low algebraic degree per round, and a standard tool in the lightweight-cryptography cryptanalysis toolkit (relevant to Ascon-class scrutiny).
- **Rotational cryptanalysis**: tracks how a *rotational pair* of inputs (x, x rotated by some amount) propagates through ARX operations, exploiting the fact that bitwise rotation commutes with XOR and (approximately, with a known bias) with modular addition — the natural cryptanalytic counterpart to ARX ciphers specifically (Salsa20/ChaCha20-family designs are evaluated against this attack class as a matter of course).
- **Invariant attacks**: find a nonlinear (or affine) subspace/property of the state that is *preserved* by the round function regardless of the key — most famously and damagingly against certain lightweight ciphers and specific weak-key classes of some SPN designs (e.g., published invariant-subspace attacks against some Midori and Scream-family lightweight ciphers), a reminder that lightweight/lower-round-count designs trade margin for efficiency and need correspondingly more scrutiny per round.

### 1.4 Algebraic attacks

Represent the cipher as a system of polynomial (typically over GF(2), i.e., Boolean) equations relating key bits, plaintext, and ciphertext, then solve the system:
- **SAT-solver-based attacks**: encode the cipher as a Boolean satisfiability instance and hand it to an off-the-shelf modern SAT solver (CryptoMiniSat and similar tools built specifically with cryptanalysis in mind). Effective mainly against reduced-round or structurally weak ciphers/hash functions; a standard, practical tool in the applied cryptanalyst's toolkit precisely because modern SAT solvers are surprisingly powerful on cryptographically-structured instances even though SAT is NP-complete in general.
- **MILP (Mixed-Integer Linear Programming)-based attacks**: rather than solving the cipher outright, use MILP solvers to *search for optimal differential/linear characteristics* automatically — this has become the dominant modern methodology for finding the best known differential/linear/integral distinguishers against a wide range of ciphers, largely displacing hand-crafted characteristic search for well-studied cipher families, and is standard practice in current published cryptanalysis papers.
- **Gröbner basis attacks**: solve the polynomial system directly via Gröbner basis computation (Buchberger's algorithm and modern refinements like F4/F5). The **XSL attack** (Courtois–Pieprzyk, 2002) claimed a sub-exhaustive-search algebraic attack against AES using this family of techniques; its practical validity was heavily disputed and is now generally regarded by the cryptographic community as **not actually working as claimed** at real AES parameters — an instructive, still-cited cautionary tale about the gap between an attack's asymptotic/theoretical claims and its actual, verified practical applicability, and about the field's (correct) demand for independent verification before accepting a break.

### 1.5 Side-channel and physical attacks

These attack the **implementation**, not the mathematics — arguably the single most practically important category for real-world security, since the overwhelming majority of publicly disclosed cryptographic vulnerabilities in deployed systems over the last two decades have been implementation-level, not algorithm-level breaks.

- **Timing attacks** (Kocher, 1996): measure how long an operation takes; if execution time depends on secret data (a table lookup at a key-dependent index, a branch on a secret bit, an early-exit comparison), the timing signal leaks the secret, sometimes bit-by-bit across many measurements. Classic examples: RSA's naive modular exponentiation (early-exit optimizations leaking exponent bits), non-constant-time big-number comparison (leaking MAC/signature verification results one byte at a time — the basis of many practical padding-oracle and forgery attacks), and table-lookup-based AES software implementations (cache-timing, see below).
- **Cache-timing attacks**: a specific, especially dangerous timing-attack subclass exploiting CPU cache behavior — if a secret-dependent memory access pattern causes measurably different cache hit/miss timing (Prime+Probe, Flush+Reload, and related techniques), an attacker (even one running as an unprivileged co-tenant process on the same physical machine, relevant for cloud/shared-hosting threat models) can reconstruct key bits. This is precisely why table-lookup-based software AES was considered a real practical risk before AES-NI hardware instructions became universal, and why ARX designs like ChaCha20 (no data-dependent table lookups at all) are valued as inherently cache-timing-resistant by construction.
- **Power analysis**: **Simple Power Analysis (SPA)** reads secret-dependent operation sequences directly off a power-consumption trace (e.g., distinguishing an RSA square from a multiply in a naive square-and-multiply implementation, directly revealing exponent bits); **Differential Power Analysis (DPA)** (Kocher, Jaffe, Jun, 1999) is far more powerful — it statistically correlates many power traces against hypothesized intermediate values, defeating even implementations with no *obviously* secret-dependent control flow, because power consumption of underlying transistor-level operations still correlates with the Hamming weight/distance of secret-dependent data even when the *code path* is identical. DPA is the reason **masking** (splitting every sensitive intermediate value into multiple random shares that individually reveal nothing, recombined only at the very end) became standard practice in smart-card and hardware-token cryptographic implementations, and increasingly in software implementations targeting high-assurance contexts.
- **Fault attacks**: deliberately induce a computational error (via voltage/clock glitching, laser/electromagnetic fault injection, or software-induced faults) during a cryptographic operation, then exploit the *difference* between a correct and a faulty output to recover key material. The canonical example is the **Bellcore attack** against RSA-CRT (Part 1, 2.1): a single faulted computation of one CRT branch, combined with the correct result of the other branch and the public key, yields the private factorization via one GCD computation — devastatingly simple once understood, and the reason production RSA-CRT implementations must verify their own output (re-encrypt and compare, or an equivalent check) before releasing a signature. **Differential Fault Analysis (DFA)** against symmetric ciphers (inducing faults partway through AES's rounds and comparing correct/faulted ciphertext pairs) is similarly powerful and a standard evaluated threat for hardware security modules and smart cards.
- **Microarchitectural / transient-execution attacks**: a newer (2018-onward, Spectre/Meltdown-initiated) attack class exploiting speculative and out-of-order execution in modern CPUs — the processor speculatively executes instructions past a not-yet-resolved branch or memory-permission check, and even though architecturally-visible state is rolled back on misprediction, *microarchitectural* state (cache contents, in particular) is not, leaking data that should have been protected by memory isolation boundaries entirely independent of any cryptographic algorithm's correctness. **Meltdown** (2018) exploited out-of-order execution to read privileged kernel memory from unprivileged code; **Spectre** (2018) exploited branch-prediction training to leak data across security boundaries even *within* correctly-isolated code. This attack class has continued to yield new instances every year since: recent (2025) published work specifically against Apple silicon includes **SLAP** (data-speculation attacks via load-address prediction, targeting M2-and-later chips) and **FLOP** (exploiting false load-value predictions on the Apple M3), both presented at top venues (IEEE S&P, USENIX Security) in 2025, alongside continued general research into post-barrier Spectre variants and kernel-level cross-cache attacks (e.g., SLUBStick). The practical significance for cryptography specifically: any secret-dependent code running on a shared or adversary-influenced CPU (browsers running untrusted JavaScript, cloud multi-tenant hosts, any code executing alongside less-trusted code on the same core) must be evaluated against this attack class independent of how mathematically sound its underlying cryptographic algorithm is — a genuinely open, actively-researched arms race between CPU microarchitecture designers and attackers, not a solved problem, and one that shows no sign of concluding as CPUs grow more aggressively speculative for performance reasons.
- **Related-key attacks**: assume the adversary can obtain ciphertexts under keys related by a known or chosen relationship (K, K⊕Δ) — an unusual threat model for a single cipher used correctly, but directly relevant to protocols/modes that *derive* multiple keys from a single master key via simple, structured relationships (a real, historically-exploited weakness in WEP's per-packet key derivation, and part of why modern key-derivation functions like HKDF are designed to destroy any exploitable algebraic relationship between derived keys even when the deriving inputs are related).

### 1.6 Historical examples and current research posture

The overall arc: differential and linear cryptanalysis (late 1980s–1990s) drove the design of AES and its wide-trail-strategy provable bounds; algebraic/SAT/MILP tooling (2000s–present) became the standard *automated* method for finding the best characteristics for a *given* design, largely displacing manual characteristic-search; side-channel and microarchitectural attacks (1996–present, continuously) shifted the field's center of gravity from "is the algorithm mathematically sound" (largely a solved problem for AES/SHA-2/SHA-3/ChaCha20 at this point, modulo the PQC transition) toward "is the *implementation* free of exploitable physical/timing/microarchitectural leakage," which remains very much unsolved in general and is the dominant subject of current top-venue cryptographic-hardware research (CHES — Cryptographic Hardware and Embedded Systems — being the flagship venue). The 2025–2026 developments (SLAP/FLOP on Apple silicon; the Anthropic AI-cryptanalysis result on HAWK and reduced-round AES; ongoing NIST PQC-candidate side-channel evaluation, especially around Falcon/FN-DSA's Gaussian sampling) all sit squarely in this continuing arc — cryptanalysis, as a research activity, has arguably never been *more* active than it is right now, even though the "big" mathematical primitives (AES, SHA-2/3) haven't meaningfully moved in over a decade.

## 2. Randomness

Randomness is not a peripheral concern — it is a **load-bearing cryptographic primitive** in its own right, and failures here have caused some of the most severe real-world cryptographic breaks in history, independent of how strong the surrounding algorithms were.

### 2.1 Entropy sources

**True randomness** must ultimately come from a physical process with genuine unpredictability: thermal/Johnson-Nyquist noise, quantum-mechanical effects, or — carefully and controversially — timing jitter in system events (interrupt timing, disk seek timing historically, now largely superseded by dedicated hardware). Hardware RNGs built into modern CPUs — **RDRAND** and **RDSEED** on x86 (Intel/AMD), providing respectively a conditioned/whitened output stream and raw seed-quality entropy — are now the standard baseline entropy source on server/desktop hardware, though their *opacity* (the entropy-generation circuit itself is not independently auditable by software) has been a recurring point of controversy, especially after the Dual_EC_DRBG revelations (2.4 below) heightened general suspicion of any closed-source, vendor-controlled randomness component regardless of specific evidence against RDRAND itself. **Jitter entropy** (measuring fine-grained timing variation in CPU execution, e.g., the widely-used `jitterentropy` library integrated into the Linux kernel's RNG) is a software-only entropy source used specifically to avoid depending solely on a single hardware vendor's opaque implementation — a defense-in-depth response to exactly that controversy.

### 2.2 Extraction and conditioning

Raw physical entropy sources are rarely *uniformly* random even when unpredictable — they have bias and correlation. **Randomness extraction** (via the **leftover hash lemma**, using a universal hash function keyed by a public seed) converts a high-min-entropy but non-uniform source into an output statistically close to uniform, with a provable bound on the remaining statistical distance as a function of input min-entropy and desired output length — this is the formal information-theoretic tool underlying essentially every real entropy-conditioning step in a production RNG pipeline (Part 1, 2.5's min-entropy concept is exactly what's being extracted from here).

### 2.3 CSPRNGs and DRBGs

Once conditioned, a small amount of true entropy (a **seed**) is *stretched* into arbitrarily much pseudorandom output via a **cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator (CSPRNG)**, formally called a **DRBG (Deterministic Random Bit Generator)** in NIST's SP 800-90A terminology:
- **CTR_DRBG**: runs a block cipher (typically AES) in counter mode as the pseudorandom generation engine, reseeded periodically from the true entropy source; the most widely deployed NIST-standardized DRBG, valued for reusing well-analyzed, hardware-accelerated block-cipher machinery.
- **Hash_DRBG / HMAC_DRBG**: analogous constructions built from a hash function or HMAC instead of a block cipher.
- **ChaCha20-based DRBGs**: increasingly common in software contexts (notably the modern **Linux kernel RNG**, which switched its core CSPRNG mechanism to a ChaCha20-based design) for the same software-speed and side-channel-friendliness reasons ChaCha20 is favored as an AEAD component (Part 2).
- **Fortuna** (Ferguson, Schneier, 2003): a carefully-engineered CSPRNG design specifically addressing entropy-pool-management failure modes observed in earlier generators (like the original Linux `/dev/random`'s ad hoc entropy-estimation approach) — uses multiple entropy pools with staggered reseeding schedules specifically to remain secure even if some entropy sources are partially compromised or an attacker can influence some (but not all) inputs, and to recover automatically after a state compromise given enough fresh entropy, without requiring an explicit "am I healed yet" signal the way some earlier designs implicitly needed.
- **Yarrow** (Kelsey, Schneier, Ferguson, 1999): Fortuna's direct conceptual predecessor, used historically in macOS/FreeBSD's `/dev/random` implementations before later replacement by more modern designs.
- **Windows RNG (CNG's `BCryptGenRandom`)**: Microsoft's current-generation CSPRNG stack, integrated with hardware RNG sources where available and NIST SP 800-90A-compliant DRBG mechanisms.

### 2.4 Historical RNG failures — the field's cautionary tales

- **Dual_EC_DRBG**: a NIST-standardized (SP 800-90A, 2006) DRBG based on elliptic-curve arithmetic, cryptographically unusual among DRBGs for using number-theoretic (rather than symmetric-primitive) structure. Researchers (Shumow and Ferguson, 2007) publicly demonstrated that the specific constant points defining the algorithm's elliptic-curve parameters *could* have been chosen (by whoever generated them) such that knowledge of a secret relationship between the two points would let that party predict all future outputs from a small number of observed outputs — an algorithmic **backdoor**, structurally possible by design, years before it was confirmed to have actually been exploited. The 2013 Snowden disclosures, combined with reporting that RSA Security's BSAFE library had used Dual_EC_DRBG as its default RNG under an alleged $10 million NSA contract, substantiated long-standing suspicion that this theoretical backdoor had been real and had been deliberately engineered into a widely-deployed commercial cryptographic product. NIST formally withdrew Dual_EC_DRBG from its standards in 2014. This episode is arguably the single most consequential cautionary tale in the entire randomness sub-field, and a major driver of the field's subsequent emphasis on transparent, auditable, "nothing-up-my-sleeve" design (directly echoing the Curve25519/NIST-curve provenance debate from Part 3) and of increased general wariness toward closed standardization processes for anything touching randomness generation specifically.
- **Debian OpenSSL vulnerability (2006–2008)**: a well-intentioned but catastrophic patch to Debian's OpenSSL package accidentally removed nearly all sources of entropy feeding the seeding process (a Valgrind/Purify warning-suppression change that inadvertently commented out the actual entropy-gathering code, not just the tool-warning-triggering line), reducing the effective key space for every key generated on affected systems for about two years to a tiny, enumerable set (effectively parameterized mainly by process ID). This produced trivially brute-forceable SSH keys, SSL certificates, and OpenVPN keys across a huge number of Debian and Ubuntu systems once discovered in 2008 — a pure software-engineering failure with zero cryptographic-algorithm involvement, and a standing illustration of why RNG code deserves *more*, not less, scrutiny and change-control caution than ordinary code.
- **Sony PS3 ECDSA nonce reuse** (Part 3, 1.4): not strictly a *randomness generation* failure in the entropy-source sense, but a nonce-*management* failure with the identical mathematical consequence — worth cross-referencing as the signature-scheme-specific instance of "randomness reuse is catastrophic."
- **Android SecureRandom Bitcoin wallet incidents (2013)**: a flaw in certain versions of Android's `SecureRandom` implementation (related to improper PRNG seeding after a Java Cryptography Architecture provider misconfiguration) led to insufficiently random ECDSA signature nonces in several popular Bitcoin wallet apps, enabling real, executed theft of funds from affected wallets — another concrete instance of the same fundamental failure mode (weak/predictable nonce → private key recovery) recurring in a completely different software stack a few years after the PS3 incident, underscoring that this is a *pattern*, not a one-off.

### 2.5 Testing randomness — and its limits

- **NIST STS (Statistical Test Suite, SP 800-22)**: a standard battery of statistical tests (frequency, runs, spectral, matrix-rank, and many others) applied to a bit sequence to detect deviations from expected random behavior.
- **Dieharder**: an extended, actively-maintained open-source test battery building on the older "Diehard" tests (Marsaglia) plus STS-derived and additional tests.
- **TestU01** (L'Ecuyer, Simard): widely regarded as the most rigorous and comprehensive open academic randomness-testing suite, including the demanding "BigCrush" battery that has been the tool of choice for exposing subtle weaknesses in several well-known non-cryptographic PRNGs (Mersenne Twister fails some BigCrush tests, for instance, despite being an excellent *statistical* PRNG for simulation purposes — a useful reminder that statistical-quality PRNGs and cryptographically-secure PRNGs are different design goals, and passing statistical tests is necessary but nowhere near sufficient for cryptographic security).
- **PractRand**: a newer, actively-developed test suite specifically designed to be more sensitive (able to detect subtler flaws with less input data) than older batteries, and to test at very large sample sizes to catch weaknesses that only manifest after enormous amounts of output — has become a preferred tool in the amateur and academic PRNG-design community for exactly this sensitivity.

**The fundamental limit, and the single most important thing to understand about randomness testing**: statistical test suites can only detect **statistical deviation from uniform**, and a well-designed CSPRNG's output is, by construction, computationally indistinguishable from random precisely because it's designed to have *no* detectable statistical structure — meaning **every one of these test suites will happily certify a completely compromised RNG as "random" if the compromise is a computational/cryptographic weakness rather than a statistical one.** The canonical illustration: Dual_EC_DRBG's backdoored output passes every standard statistical randomness test with flying colors — its outputs *are* statistically indistinguishable from random to anyone without the secret relationship between its curve points, which is exactly what makes it a dangerous backdoor rather than an obviously-broken generator. This is a frequently-misunderstood point even among competent engineers: **"passes randomness tests" is not evidence of cryptographic security, only of the much weaker property of statistical uniformity** — the actual security of a CSPRNG rests on cryptographic hardness assumptions and design review (ideally including provenance transparency, per the Dual_EC_DRBG lesson), not on any test suite's output.

### 2.6 Common misconceptions about randomness

- "It passed Dieharder/TestU01, so it's cryptographically secure" — false, per 2.5 above; statistical testing and cryptographic security are different properties.
- "More entropy sources mixed together is always safer" — true in general (Fortuna's multi-pool design exploits exactly this), but only if the mixing function itself is cryptographically sound; naively XORing multiple weak sources together does not reliably produce a strong one unless at least one source is genuinely high-entropy and the combination function is a proper extractor.
- "Hardware RNGs (RDRAND) are inherently trustworthy because they're hardware" — the Dual_EC_DRBG and general closed-source-component-trust concerns apply regardless of whether the untrusted component is implemented in hardware or software; hardware opacity is, if anything, *harder* to independently audit than software, which is exactly why software-only entropy sources like jitter entropy are deliberately kept in the mix rather than trusting hardware RNGs alone.
- "A CSPRNG needs constant re-seeding from true entropy to stay secure" — mostly false for well-designed DRBGs between reseeds (their security relies on the pseudorandomness of the underlying cryptographic primitive stretching a single good seed indefinitely, up to specified reseed intervals meant to bound the damage of eventual state compromise, not because the pseudorandom stretching itself "wears out" statistically).
