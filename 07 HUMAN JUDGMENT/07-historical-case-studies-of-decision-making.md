# 07 — Historical Case Studies of Decision-Making

*Part of a multi-file report — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map. Assumes `01`–`06`. Technical facts about every program below are covered in full in companion history report; this file's distinct contribution is rereading the same facts specifically through this report's decision-making framework.*

---

## The AES Competition — a kill criterion applied to an entire field's incumbent assumption

**The decision, reread through `01`'s framework**: NIST's 1997 choice to run an open competition rather than commission a design was, itself, a strategic bet on process over any specific technical hypothesis — restating companion history report `01`'s finding that this decision was genuinely uncertain at the time, not an obvious call, given DES's substantially closed development. **The kill criterion this program implicitly applied, reconstructed**: any finalist candidate found vulnerable to a practical attack during the multi-round public cryptanalysis period would be eliminated regardless of its other merits — Serpent's larger security margin didn't override this evaluation once every finalist cleared the practical-attack bar, and the final decision then rested on `01` §2's fuller value calculation (margin *and* performance *and* the wide-trail strategy's provable-bound advantage) rather than on cryptanalytic survival alone, which every finalist had already achieved. **The lesson this program teaches about problem selection specifically**: NIST's decision to weigh multiple criteria jointly, rather than optimizing for a single axis (maximum margin), directly anticipated `01` §2.1's three-factor value framework by roughly three decades.

## The SHA-3 Competition — a negative result driving a positive research program

**The decision**: opening a second competition despite SHA-2 never having actually failed is this report's cleanest real-world instance of `04` §3's finding that a *negative* result (or, more precisely here, an unresolved theoretical *concern* — the Merkle–Damgård structural unease companion history report `02` documents) can drive a valuable, field-changing positive research program even without a confirmed break. **The kill criterion, reconstructed**: had Keccak's sponge construction, once selected, subsequently been found weaker than SHA-2 rather than merely structurally different, the entire program's premise would have been undermined — this didn't happen, but the program's designers (per companion history report `03`) explicitly built in exactly this risk assessment as part of the sponge construction's rigorous, from-first-principles indifferentiability proof, rather than proceeding on structural novelty alone.

## CAESAR — a competition that ended without a single unambiguous winner, and what that teaches about success criteria

**The decision worth examining specifically**: unlike AES and SHA-3, CAESAR concluded with a **portfolio** of recommended algorithms for different use cases, not one single winner — worth rereading through `02` §1.5's success-criteria discipline: this outcome reflects the program having defined success, from early on, as "identify strong options across several genuinely different deployment profiles" rather than "identify the single best AEAD scheme," a success criterion NIST's later Lightweight Cryptography competition (companion design-process report `05`'s Ascon-over-Sparkle case study) would echo in its own multi-axis evaluation, but CAESAR's explicit portfolio structure makes this most visible.

## NIST PQC — the clearest real-world example of `02`'s dependency-management discipline

**The decision sequence, reread through `02` §2.4**: NIST's staged approach — primary standards (ML-KEM, ML-DSA, SLH-DSA) finalized first, with HQC and FN-DSA explicitly following as later, independently-timed hedges — is a direct, real-world instance of ordering a multi-year program's phases so later phases depend on earlier phases' *methodology* (the open, multi-round competition process itself, now validated across three prior iterations) rather than on any specific *result* the earlier phases produced. **The explicit risk-anticipation this program demonstrates**: companion ARX report `03` §2.3's documentation of NIST's stated rationale for HQC specifically as a hedge against a future lattice-cryptography break is `02` §2.5's "anticipate dead ends explicitly, before they happen" discipline, executed at the scale of an entire national standards process — NIST didn't wait for lattice cryptography to actually fail before funding a structurally-independent backup; the risk was named and planned against years in advance.

## ChaCha's development — voluntary refinement as a case study in `01` §3's novelty discipline

**The decision worth rereading**: companion history report `04` and companion design-process report `05`'s finding that ChaCha's 2008 redesign was analysis-driven refinement, not crisis response, is this report's cleanest illustration of `01` §3.1's novelty-concentration principle in practice — Bernstein didn't redesign Salsa20's entire architecture; he identified one specific, narrow axis (topology/diffusion-rate) where genuine improvement was both possible and valuable, and left every other design decision (word size, operation set, general structure) untouched, exactly the "concentrate novelty at one carefully-chosen point" discipline this report has argued for throughout.

## Ascon's development — a success criterion that wasn't "win the competition"

**The decision, reread through `01` §1.3's load-bearing-assumption test**: Ascon's design team's actual research contribution — a genuinely well-analyzed, formally-scrutinized sponge-SPN-hybrid lightweight primitive — remained valuable and citable regardless of the eventual competition outcome, precisely because the team's own implicit success criteria (per `02` §1.5) were almost certainly framed around "produce a design that clears every rigor bar the field's evaluation apparatus applies," not "win NIST's specific selection," a distinction companion design-process report `08` §3.5 shows mattering directly: Sparkle's team, per that same case study, achieved comparable research success (the long-trail strategy's lasting citation impact) despite Ascon, not Sparkle, being NIST's eventual choice — both programs' actual success criteria, correctly specified in advance, were met, even though only one design was ultimately standardized.

## BLAKE's evolution — a program that explicitly revisited its own architecture-level decision

**The decision**: BLAKE2's optimization-focused refinement of BLAKE, followed by BLAKE3's more radical Merkle-tree architectural restructuring (companion design-process report `05`), is this report's clearest real-world instance of `02` §2.3's milestone-review discipline correctly identifying that a performance ceiling lived at the *architecture* layer, not the round-function layer — restating companion design-process report `02` §2.4's finding directly: the team recognized, correctly, that no amount of further round-function tuning (BLAKE2's own approach) would remove BLAKE3's targeted parallelism ceiling, and chose to revisit the higher-level decision rather than continuing to invest in a lower-level fix that couldn't, even in principle, solve the actual problem — exactly the "correctly diagnose which process layer a problem lives at" discipline that companion report names as a distinct, generalizable skill.

## The Synthesized Lesson Across All Six Programs

Every program in this file, reread through this report's framework, shows the same underlying pattern: **explicit, stated success criteria and risk-hedging decisions, made early and revisited honestly as evidence accumulated** — not, in any of these six cases, a program that simply pursued its original plan unmodified until an outcome arrived. AES weighed multiple criteria jointly from the outset; SHA-3 hedged against an unrealized risk; CAESAR redefined "winning" as a portfolio; NIST PQC staged its dependencies explicitly; ChaCha concentrated its novelty deliberately; BLAKE3 correctly re-diagnosed which layer its problem actually lived at. None of these decisions were obviously "correct" in advance — they were, in every case, genuine bets made under real uncertainty, using exactly the disciplines `01`–`06` of this report describe in the abstract.

Part `08` (`08-independent-research-and-the-decision-framework.md`), the final file, turns from institutional programs to what an independent researcher specifically can realistically do, and consolidates this entire report into one usable decision-making framework.
