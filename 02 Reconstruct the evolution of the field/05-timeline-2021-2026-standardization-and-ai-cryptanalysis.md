# 05 — 2021–2026: Standardization, and Cryptanalysis Gets an AI Collaborator

*Part of a multi-file chronological history — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map and methodology. This chapter closes the chronological account; Parts `06`–`08` step back to cross-cutting analysis.*

---

## The state of the field entering 2021

The PQC standardization process was in its later rounds, differential-neural cryptanalysis was two years old and still establishing itself, Sparkle/Alzette had just given ARX its long-trail-strategy provable margin, and the NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition was heading toward its own conclusion. Nothing yet suggested how eventful the next five years would be.

## 2021 — Deep-dissection of what neural distinguishers actually learn

**Benamira, Gerault, Peyrin, and Tan published "A Deeper Look at Machine Learning-Based Cryptanalysis"** at EUROCRYPT, alongside Gohr's own continuing follow-up work (the "Real Differences Experiment" and "aaaa-blinding" experiments), specifically interrogating *what* the neural distinguishers introduced in 2019 were actually exploiting — real structure beyond the classical differential distribution table, or a repackaging of already-known statistical signal. **Why this methodological self-scrutiny mattered**: it's a clean instance of a recurring field norm this history has traced since the ROM critique of 2004 — when a new, powerful technique arrives, the field's next move is almost always to rigorously interrogate *why* it works, not just deploy it, and this particular question (how much of differential-neural power is genuinely novel) remained explicitly unresolved through 2025, a live methodological debate this history's final chapter inherits rather than closes.

## 2022 — SIKE falls, catastrophically and classically

**Wouter Castryck and Thomas Decru published an efficient key-recovery attack on SIDH** (the isogeny-based key-exchange scheme underlying SIKE, then a fourth-round NIST PQC candidate), building on Ernst Kani's decades-old theorem — and the attack was **classical**, not quantum: an ordinary laptop could recover SIKE's secret isogeny in about an hour, for parameters previously believed to offer well over 100 bits of security. **Why this was one of the most dramatic breaks in this entire 33-year history, not just a PQC footnote**: SIKE had already survived four full rounds of NIST's open, globally-scrutinized competition process — the exact process this history has repeatedly credited with producing trustworthy standards — and it still fell, completely, to a technique that had been mathematically available (Kani's theorem long predates SIKE) but simply hadn't been connected to the attack until this paper. **The lesson the field explicitly took from this**, and one this report's companion documents cite repeatedly: surviving many competition rounds is strong evidence of scrutiny, not proof of security, and "young" mathematical foundations — even ones that have been formally competing for years — deserve continued, not diminishing, suspicion.

In the same year, NIST selected the PQC program's primary standards — **Kyber (lattice-based KEM), Dilithium (lattice-based signatures), Falcon (NTRU-lattice signatures), and SPHINCS+ (hash-based signatures)** — for standardization, explicitly citing the desire for a hash-based fallback (SPHINCS+) specifically *because* its security touches nothing but hash-function collision resistance, no novel algebraic hardness assumption at all — the same "diversify the underlying hard-problem structure" instinct this history has now traced from SHA-3's sponge-vs-Merkle–Damgård diversification (2007–2012) through to this decision.

Also this year, **NIST selected Ascon** as the winner of its Lightweight Cryptography competition (opened 2018), over ARX-based Sparkle and AND-RX-based Xoodyak — an SPN-sponge hybrid whose internal nonlinearity comes from a fixed 5-bit substitution, not modular addition. **Why Ascon, and not the provably-margined Sparkle**: per NIST's own comparative evaluation, the decision weighed the full evaluation criteria — hardware and software footprint, side-channel posture, API flexibility, implementation maturity — not security margin alone, and Ascon's package won on that fuller balance despite Sparkle's comparably rigorous, methodologically novel long-trail-strategy security case. This is this history's cleanest illustration that a provable security margin, however genuine and hard-won, is necessary but not sufficient for standardization success — a real, sobering data point for any researcher (including, explicitly, the audience of this report's companion ARX-specific document) hoping a rigorous design alone guarantees adoption.

## 2023 — Chinese and European automated-search tooling accelerates, and PQXDH ships early

**Bellini, Gerault, and collaborators published CLAASP** (a unified, open-source automated-analysis library letting a researcher define a new symmetric primitive once and automatically generate MILP/SAT security-evaluation models against it), and **Coutinho and Peyrin published "Boosting differential-linear cryptanalysis of ChaCha7 with MILP"** at ToSC — the first application of MILP-based automation specifically to *optimizing the differential-linear distinguisher itself*, not just pure differential trails, directly extending the ChaCha attack lineage opened by Beierle, Leander, and Todo in 2020. Separately, **Coutinho, Passos, Grados Vásquez, and collaborators published "Latin Dances Reloaded"** at ASIACRYPT, refining the Probabilistic Neutral Bits methodology underlying essentially the entire ChaCha/Salsa20 attack line since 2008, and proposing a new cipher, Forró, as a design-side response — a pattern (cryptanalysis directly informing a fresh design proposal) this history has seen before (DES's S-boxes responding to differential cryptanalysis before the technique was even public) and would see again.

**Signal deployed PQXDH**, replacing X3DH's pure-ECDH initial handshake with a hybrid combining classical X25519 and (pre-standardization) Kyber — a deliberate, publicly-explained decision to deploy PQC ahead of NIST's formal finalization, given the urgency of the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat model for long-lived conversation confidentiality, accepting some algorithm-agility risk in exchange for earlier protection. This is the first major real-world deployment of PQC key establishment in this entire history, arriving a full year before NIST's own standards were finalized.

## 2024 — FIPS 203/204/205 finalize, and Apple ships continuous post-quantum protection

**NIST finalized FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, formerly Kyber), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, formerly Dilithium), and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, formerly SPHINCS+)** on August 13, 2024 — closing an eight-year, multi-round open competition (opened 2016, Part `04`), the fourth and most consequential-to-date reuse of the AES-competition institutional template this history has now traced across four separate standardization efforts (AES, SHA-3, Lightweight Cryptography, PQC). **Apple shipped PQ3** in iOS 17.4, weaving Kyber/ML-KEM into iMessage's *ongoing* per-message ratchet, not just its initial handshake — describing it as reaching a self-defined "Level 3" continuous post-quantum protection, a genuinely new bar for what "post-quantum secure messaging" means, going beyond PQXDH's 2023 handshake-only protection. **Chrome enabled hybrid X25519MLKEM768 by default** from version 124, beginning the still-ongoing (as of Part `05`'s writing) real-world TLS hybrid-PQC rollout.

## 2025 — HQC, Ascon's final standard, Signal's Triple Ratchet, and the Window Heuristic

**NIST selected HQC** (code-based) on March 11, 2025 as a structurally-independent backup KEM to ML-KEM — the explicit, direct institutional answer to the risk that a single future breakthrough against structured lattices could otherwise simultaneously compromise both ML-KEM and ML-DSA, since both rest on the same underlying hard-problem family. **NIST finalized Ascon as SP 800-232** on August 13, 2025, closing the Lightweight Cryptography competition's institutional arc that began in 2018 (Part `04`) and had selected Ascon over Sparkle/Xoodyak in 2022.

**Signal deployed the Triple Ratchet (SPQR, Sparse Post-Quantum Ratchet)**, extending PQXDH's 2023 handshake-only protection into a second, continuous, parallel post-quantum ratchet running alongside the classical Double Ratchet for the *entire* conversation lifecycle — matching, and in its formal security-proof rigor (published at Eurocrypt and USENIX Security 2025) arguably exceeding, Apple's PQ3 continuous-protection bar set the previous year. **Bellini, Gerault, Grados, and Peyrin published the "Window Heuristic"** at CT-RSA, the most recent major advance in modular-addition MILP/SAT modeling specifically, finding the first 4-round ChaCha differential path with probability above 2^-256. In parallel, a wave of **automated rotational-XOR (RX) cryptanalysis tooling** matured substantially, extending best-known RX-differential characteristics against Speck-family targets from 13 rounds (the prior hand-derived best) to 17–24 rounds via new SAT/CNF-based automated search frameworks — RX-cryptanalysis, founded in 2016 (Part `04`), finally achieving the same automated-tooling maturity MILP-based differential/linear search had achieved roughly a decade earlier.

**Flórez-Gutiérrez and Todo published "Improved Cryptanalysis of ChaCha: Beating PNBs with Bit Puncturing"** at EUROCRYPT, the most recent published advance in the ChaCha attack lineage traced continuously through this history since 2008 (Aumasson et al.'s original PNB technique) through 2020 (Beierle-Leander-Todo), 2023 (Coutinho-Peyrin, Bellini et al., Dey-Garai-Maitra), and 2024 (Xu-Xu-Tan-Qi's 7.5-round result) — pushing the best-known attack to 7.5 of ChaCha's full 20 rounds, still nowhere close to threatening the deployed cipher, but the field's most current data point on a seventeen-year-and-counting research thread.

## 2026 — Resource estimates compress, and an AI finds a real attack

Through the first half of 2026, quantum-hardware resource estimates for breaking RSA-2048/ECC-256-class parameters via Shor's algorithm underwent a sharp, largely *algorithmic* (not hardware-driven) compression — published circuit-construction improvements (including work specifically revising down the logical-qubit requirements for 256-bit elliptic curves) pushed widely-cited estimates from roughly 20 million physical qubits a few years prior toward 1 million, then toward the 100,000–10,000 range by mid-2026, according to multiple independently-tracked sources, even as the largest actual quantum processors (Google's Willow, roughly 105 physical qubits) remained many orders of magnitude short of any of these figures. **Why this mattered as a historical inflection, distinct from the underlying hardware not having actually changed**: several serious practitioners publicly revised their own risk timelines upward specifically in response to the *rate* at which these algorithmic estimates had been falling (each revision arriving faster than the last), a distinctly different, and to some observers more alarming, category of progress than steady hardware scaling — because it can happen suddenly, via a single clever paper, rather than gradually, via years of engineering. Google's own internal PQC migration target moved to 2029, explicitly citing this trend.

Then, **on July 28, 2026, Anthropic disclosed that its Claude Mythos Preview model — working largely autonomously, with periodic human direction and, crucially, hundreds of hours of independent human verification before publication — had discovered a previously unknown mathematical symmetry in the lattice structure of HAWK**, a lattice-based signature scheme then in round 3 of NIST's "additional digital signatures" standardization track, yielding a genuine key-recovery attack halving HAWK's claimed security margin. The same research effort separately produced a 200–800× speedup on the best previously known meet-in-the-middle attack against 7-round (of 10) AES-128 — an academically weakened variant, not the deployed cipher. **The HAWK development team withdrew their algorithm from NIST consideration as a direct, immediate consequence** — a real, standards-altering outcome, not a hypothetical.

**Why this closes this 33-year history on the note it does**: read against the full arc this report has traced, the HAWK event is not, in this report's assessment, best understood as a sudden rupture. It sits at the end of a long, continuous automation lineage this history has followed explicitly — hand-derived differential/linear cryptanalysis (1990–2011) giving way to MILP/SAT-automated trail search (2011–2020) giving way to differential-neural cryptanalysis (2019–2025) — and the HAWK event is, in a structural sense, simply the next rung: a system conducting sustained, semi-autonomous mathematical research over many hours, rather than executing a single automated search or a single trained model's inference. What is genuinely new, and genuinely unresolved as this history closes, is the *degree of sustained autonomy* involved, and the open question — named explicitly in this report's companion general-cryptography document, and inherited here unresolved — of whether this changes how much scrutiny a "young" cryptographic design needs before the field extends it the kind of trust AES or ChaCha20 have earned over their multi-decade, thousands-of-researcher-hours histories.

## What this era established, and where the story stands as this report is written

By August 2026, the field has: finalized its first generation of post-quantum standards after an eight-year open process, with a second wave (HQC, FN-DSA) still finalizing; watched SIKE's catastrophic, classical fall vindicate every prior lesson this history has drawn about competition survival not equaling security; watched Ascon's Lightweight Cryptography win demonstrate, again, that provable margin alone doesn't guarantee standardization; watched secure messaging move decisively from handshake-only to continuous post-quantum protection (Apple PQ3, Signal's Triple Ratchet); watched automated-search tooling mature further still (CLAASP, the Window Heuristic, tooled RX-cryptanalysis); watched quantum-hardware resource estimates compress sharply enough to visibly shift serious practitioners' risk timelines; and watched, days before this report's writing, the field's first verified, standards-altering result from a largely autonomous AI cryptanalytic research effort. This last event is, deliberately, where this chronological account ends rather than resolves — not because the story is over, but because it genuinely isn't yet, and this report's honest position, consistent throughout, is to say so plainly rather than force a tidy ending the field itself does not yet have.
