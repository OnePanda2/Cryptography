# 03 — 2009–2015: SHA-3, Curve25519, and the Snowden Reckoning

*Part of a multi-file chronological history — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map and methodology.*

---

## The state of the field entering 2009

The SHA-3 competition was underway, ARX had a proven, if still comparatively young, track record via Salsa20/ChaCha, and the field's confidence in classical public-key cryptography (RSA, ECC) remained essentially unshaken — Shor's algorithm was fifteen years old and still purely theoretical in its practical implications. Nothing yet suggested how dramatically curve-provenance trust would become a live issue within the next several years.

## 2009 — eSTREAM concludes, and Gentry breaks FHE open

The **eSTREAM competition** (launched 2004, specifically in response to the failure of several earlier stream-cipher proposals and general unease about stream-cipher design maturity relative to block ciphers) concluded, selecting a software portfolio (including Salsa20/12, a reduced-round Salsa20 variant, validating Bernstein's earlier practice of offering explicitly-named weaker variants as public cryptanalysis targets) and a separate hardware-oriented portfolio. This is the moment ARX-based stream ciphers received their first major, competition-validated institutional endorsement, directly setting up ChaCha's later dominance.

Separately, and in a different research thread entirely, **Craig Gentry published the first fully homomorphic encryption construction**, via ideal lattices and a "bootstrapping" technique letting accumulated ciphertext noise be periodically refreshed, enabling unlimited-depth computation directly on encrypted data for the first time. **Why this was electrifying and why it didn't immediately change anything practical**: FHE had been an open theoretical question since the late 1970s (Rivest, Adleman, and Dertouzos had posed it explicitly in 1978); Gentry's construction was a genuine, first-of-its-kind existence proof — but it was also spectacularly, impractically slow (performance estimates at the time put basic operations at many orders of magnitude slower than plaintext computation), setting up a seventeen-year (and still ongoing, as of Part `05`) performance-improvement research program before FHE would see its first narrow production deployments.

## 2010 — AES-NI ships, partially resolving the problem ARX was invented to solve

**Intel shipped AES-NI**, dedicated hardware instructions for AES's core operations, directly eliminating the cache-timing vulnerability that had motivated Salsa20's creation five years earlier — for any platform with the new silicon. **Why this didn't kill ARX's momentum**: AES-NI was an x86-server/desktop-first rollout; mobile chipsets, embedded microcontrollers, and a large swath of the world's actual deployed computing hardware would not have equivalent acceleration for years, in some categories not ever — meaning the *specific* problem ARX solved (fast, safe symmetric cryptography without dedicated hardware) remained live and real for a large fraction of real-world deployment targets, even as it stopped being universal. This is the year the field's cryptography landscape genuinely bifurcates into "has hardware acceleration" and "doesn't," a split that persists, largely along the same lines, through 2026.

## 2011 — MILP arrives as a cryptanalysis tool

**Mouha, Wang, Gu, and Preneel** published the field's first application of Mixed-Integer Linear Programming to automatically count the minimum number of active S-boxes in a differential trail — reframing what had always been a hand-craft search problem as a mechanically-solvable optimization problem a commercial solver (Gurobi/CPLEX) could handle exhaustively and provably-optimally. **Why this mattered far beyond its immediate technical scope**: this is the moment automated cryptanalysis tooling begins its rise from a niche technique to what would become, within roughly a decade, the field's *expected, mandatory* form of evidence for any serious new cipher's security claims — a methodological shift as consequential, in its own quieter way, as any single cipher break in this history, and one whose full consequences (an entire automated-search research ecosystem, covered in depth in this report's companion ARX-specific document) wouldn't be fully realized for another decade-plus.

## 2012 — Keccak wins SHA-3, and BLAKE2 refines the ARX hash lineage

**NIST selected Keccak** (Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters, Van Assche) as SHA-3, over strong finalists including BLAKE, Grøstl, JH, and Skein — the sponge construction's decisive institutional validation, and the moment "hash function" and "AEAD-capable duplex construction" begin converging conceptually as two API surfaces over the same underlying permutation-based primitive, a unification that would directly shape the entire NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition's design landscape years later (Part `04`). **Why Keccak specifically, and not BLAKE** (which many observers considered a strong, ARX-lineage, fast alternative): Keccak's structural novelty relative to SHA-2 — the entire point of running a *second* competition after SHA-2 hadn't actually failed — mattered more to NIST's stated criteria than BLAKE's comparably strong security margin and faster software performance in some benchmarks, a direct institutional preference for *diversity of underlying hard-problem structure* over raw performance, worth noting as a recurring theme (it recurs explicitly, and even more starkly, in the PQC program's HQC-as-backup-to-lattices decision, Part `05`).

In parallel, **Aumasson, Neves, Wilcox-O'Hearn, and Winnerlein published BLAKE2**, an aggressively performance-optimized descendant of the SHA-3-finalist BLAKE, explicit about its dual goal: beat MD5 on raw software speed while matching SHA-3-level security margins. BLAKE2's core "G function" descends directly from ChaCha's quarter-round — a clean, citable instance of ARX ideas propagating from stream-cipher design into hash-function design, the paradigm proving itself general-purpose rather than confined to its original stream-cipher use case.

## 2013 — Snowden, and NSA publishes Speck/Simon

This is the single most consequential year in this entire history for the field's *institutional* and *trust* dynamics, even though — in a narrow technical sense — no cipher was broken and no new mathematical technique was introduced.

**Edward Snowden's disclosures**, beginning in June 2013, included documents substantiating that RSA Security's BSAFE library had used Dual_EC_DRBG as its *default* random-number generator, reportedly under a lucrative NSA contract — turning Shumow and Ferguson's 2007 *theoretical* backdoor-possibility demonstration into strong evidence of an *actual, deployed, exploited* backdoor in widely-used commercial cryptographic software. **The consequences cascaded through the rest of this history**: NIST formally withdrew Dual_EC_DRBG from its standards the following year (2014); the field's tolerance for closed-provenance, unauditable parameter generation of *any* kind collapsed, essentially permanently; Curve25519 (2006), whose primary selling point until this moment had been speed and implementation-safety, suddenly acquired a second, in practice now-dominant, selling point — transparent, reproducible, "nothing up my sleeve" generation — and its adoption curve visibly steepens in the years immediately following; and the entire subsequent PQC standardization program (opened three years later, Part `04`) would be run with a level of process-transparency and open-competition rigor directly, explicitly shaped by the lesson this episode taught the field about what happens when that transparency is absent.

In a separate, and in retrospect strikingly-timed, development the very same year, **the NSA published Speck and Simon**, a matched pair of lightweight ARX (Speck, software-optimized) and AND-RX (Simon, hardware-optimized) block cipher families, released openly with an explicit invitation for public cryptanalysis. **Why the timing mattered, and why it cut against the ciphers regardless of their mathematical merit**: releasing new NSA-designed cryptography in the immediate aftermath of the Dual_EC/Snowden revelations, in the same calendar year, meant Speck/Simon entered public consciousness already carrying the full weight of the field's freshly-hardened, freshly-justified provenance suspicion — and this suspicion, not any cryptanalytic weakness (none was ever found against the full-round ciphers), is the documented, explicit reason ISO's standardization committee rejected Speck/Simon for its lightweight-cryptography standard five years later (2018, Part `04`). This is this history's cleanest illustration of the "provenance became part of the security model" throughline named in the Executive Summary — mathematics alone was no longer sufficient for institutional trust, and 2013 is the year that stopped being a hypothetical.

## 2014 — CAESAR opens, TLS 1.3 drafting begins, and Heartbleed

**The CAESAR competition** (Competition for Authenticated Encryption: Security, Applicability, and Robustness) opened, explicitly targeting authenticated encryption specifically — the field's growing recognition, sharpened by years of real-world padding-oracle and generic-composition vulnerabilities (Part `01`–`02`'s TLS CBC-mode history), that AEAD deserved its own dedicated, competition-scale scrutiny rather than being treated as a downstream composition of separately-designed encryption and MAC primitives. **NORX** (ARX-based, parallel-mode-capable) was among the many candidates.

**The IETF TLS working group began drafting TLS 1.3**, explicitly aiming to strip out the accumulated cruft and vulnerability surface of TLS 1.2 and earlier — static RSA key exchange (no forward secrecy), CBC-mode ciphersuites (the padding-oracle attack family's home), compression (CRIME/BREACH), and more — a multi-year process (finalized 2018, Part `04`) that would, among other things, make ChaCha20-Poly1305 a first-class, standardized default AEAD ciphersuite for the first time.

**The Heartbleed vulnerability** was disclosed — a memory-safety bug (a buffer over-read) in OpenSSL's TLS heartbeat extension implementation, letting an attacker read arbitrary adjacent heap memory, including private keys, from a huge fraction of the internet's TLS-terminating servers. **Why this belongs in this history despite being "just" a memory-safety bug, not a cryptographic-algorithm flaw**: Heartbleed is the event that most sharply crystallized, field-wide, the lesson that would recur throughout the rest of this history — that deployed cryptographic security failures are overwhelmingly implementation failures, not algorithm-mathematics failures — and it directly triggered a measurable, lasting increase in investment in fuzzing (OSS-Fuzz launched the following year, 2015), memory-safe language adoption for cryptographic code (accelerating Rust's eventual role in this space), and formal-verification tooling (HACL*, whose earliest work dates to this general period) that would compound over the following decade.

## 2015 — SHA-3 becomes FIPS 202, and Curve25519's adoption accelerates

**FIPS 202** formally standardized SHA-3/Keccak, closing the SHA-3 competition's institutional arc that began in 2007. In parallel, **Ed25519** (Bernstein, Duif, Lange, Schwabe, Yang — published 2011, but its real-world adoption curve steepens sharply across 2014–2016) began displacing NIST-curve-based ECDSA across a widening set of new protocol deployments (SSH most visibly), driven directly by the post-Snowden provenance-trust dynamic established the previous two years, combined with EdDSA's structural elimination of ECDSA's notorious nonce-reuse vulnerability class (Sony's 2010 PlayStation 3 signing-key catastrophe being the standing cautionary tale) via fully deterministic nonce derivation.

## What this era established, looking forward

By the end of 2015, the field had: institutionally validated the sponge construction (Keccak/SHA-3) as a durable, general-purpose structural paradigm now competing directly with Merkle–Damgård even for non-hash-function uses; watched a real, substantiated cryptographic backdoor (Dual_EC_DRBG) go from theoretical possibility to confirmed deployed reality, permanently raising the bar for what "trustworthy provenance" requires; watched that same episode retroactively justify NSA-authored cryptography's presumption of guilty-until-proven-innocent scrutiny, directly costing Speck/Simon institutional standardization despite their mathematical soundness; gained its first mechanically-automated cryptanalysis tooling (MILP-based trail search), setting up the following decade's full automated-search research ecosystem; watched Heartbleed crystallize "implementation, not algorithm" as the field's dominant practical-risk lesson; and entered the TLS 1.3 and CAESAR competition processes that would, over the following several years, reshape both the internet's default transport-security protocol and the field's formal understanding of authenticated encryption. Part `04` picks up with NIST's next, and most consequential-to-date, open competition: post-quantum cryptography.
