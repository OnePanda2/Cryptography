# 01 — 1993–2001: Classical Foundations to AES

*Part of a multi-file chronological history — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map and methodology.*

---

## The state of the field entering 1993

Public-key cryptography was seventeen years old (Diffie-Hellman, 1976) and RSA fifteen (1977-78), but neither was universally trusted yet in the way they would be by 2000 — RSA's export-control status was still legally contested in the US (cryptography was classified as a munition), and the field's *provable-security* apparatus — the machinery that would let researchers state and prove precisely what "secure" meant for a given construction — was still being built. Symmetric cryptography meant, in practice, DES (1977): a 56-bit-key, 16-round Feistel cipher whose key length was already a live, if not yet urgent, concern. Cryptanalysis was almost entirely a hand-craft: a strong cryptanalyst manually chained characteristics through a cipher's rounds, and there was no general, mechanical methodology for finding the *best* possible attack against a given design — you found *an* attack, and hoped, without proof, that it was close to the best one.

## 1993 — Linear cryptanalysis and the random oracle model

Two papers this year set the terms for the following decade. **Mitsuru Matsui's linear cryptanalysis** gave the field its second general statistical attack framework (after differential cryptanalysis, publicly formalized by Biham and Shamir in 1990, though — as later confirmed — known inside IBM/NSA since the 1970s and factored into DES's original S-box design). Matsui didn't just describe the technique; he *used* it to break full 16-round DES with 2^43 known plaintexts, faster than exhaustive key search — the first published, complete, practical break of a real, deployed cipher standard, and a watershed moment establishing that hand-derived statistical cryptanalysis could genuinely threaten fielded systems, not just toy reduced-round variants. **Bellare and Rogaway's "Random Oracles are Practical"** (CCS 1993) gave the field a proof *methodology* it badly needed: model a hash function as a perfect random function every party can query, and prove constructions secure relative to that idealization. This mattered because it let designers build genuinely efficient, deployable constructions (OAEP, PSS, coming in 1994/1996) with a rigorous-looking security argument, at a moment when fully standard-model provable security for efficient constructions was often out of reach. The ROM would become the field's dominant, most-used, and most-debated proof tool for the next three decades — useful immediately, contested eventually (2004, Part `02`).

**Why these mattered at the time**: DES's key length was a known, if not yet critical, weakness, and Matsui's result proved the cipher's *structure*, not just its key length, could fail against a sufficiently determined analyst — a genuinely alarming finding for a standard the entire financial and government sectors depended on. The ROM mattered because the field's public-key toolkit (RSA, Diffie-Hellman) needed padding schemes and constructions that were both efficient *and* had some rigorous security argument, and nothing else on offer delivered both.

## 1994 — RC5, Shor's algorithm, and OAEP

**Ron Rivest's RC5** introduced data-dependent rotation as a deliberate cryptographic design element — a direct ancestor, in retrospect, of the entire ARX paradigm that would come to dominate portable software cryptography a decade later (though RC5 itself is not usually classified as pure ARX in the modern taxonomic sense, since its rotation amounts are *data*-dependent rather than fixed). **Peter Shor's algorithm**, showing a sufficiently large quantum computer could factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time, was, at the time, a pure theory result with essentially zero practical urgency — quantum computers of any consequential size didn't exist and weren't remotely near existing — but it planted a flag that would not become operationally important for another two decades, and whose *eventual* consequences (Part `04`–`05`) reshaped the entire public-key landscape. **Bellare and Rogaway's OAEP** (Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding) gave RSA encryption its first widely-adopted, ROM-proven-secure padding scheme, directly responding to the well-known insecurity of "textbook RSA" (no padding at all).

**Why Shor's algorithm didn't cause immediate panic, and why that was the correct call at the time**: the gap between "a polynomial-time quantum algorithm exists in theory" and "a quantum computer capable of running it against cryptographically relevant parameters exists" was — and, as of 2026, largely still is — enormous, and nothing about 1994's quantum-hardware landscape suggested the timeline was short. The field filed this away rather than acting on it, and that turned out to be correct for roughly the next twenty years — the eventual urgency (Part `04`) arrived on its own schedule, driven by later, better resource estimates, not by 1994's theoretical result alone.

## 1995 — SHA-1 and the maturing hash-function landscape

NIST standardized **SHA-1** (a revision of the flawed original SHA, later called SHA-0) as FIPS 180-1, extending the Merkle–Damgård/Davies–Meyer hash-function lineage MD4/MD5 had established. At the time, SHA-1 was considered a solid, conservative design — its eventual, slow-motion collapse (Part `02`) was more than a decade away and not remotely anticipated in 1995.

## 1996 — Grover's algorithm and the DPA foundation

**Lov Grover's algorithm**, giving only a quadratic (not exponential) quantum speedup for unstructured search, meant symmetric cryptography's eventual quantum-era response would be comparatively mild — double the key length, done — a genuinely different, less urgent story than Shor's outright break of public-key schemes, and this asymmetry (established in 1996, still exactly the operative logic in 2026) is why the entire subsequent post-quantum research program concentrated on public-key replacement, never on replacing AES. Separately, **Paul Kocher, Joshua Jaffe, and Benjamin Jun's work on power analysis** (published fully as Differential Power Analysis in 1999, but the foundational timing-attack work began in 1996 with Kocher's "Timing Attacks on Implementations of Diffie-Hellman, RSA, DSS, and Other Systems") opened an entirely new attack surface: **the implementation, not the mathematics**. This is the moment the field's center of gravity began — slowly, and only fully realized over the following decade — to shift from "is the algorithm mathematically sound" toward "is the physical implementation free of exploitable leakage," a shift that, by 2026 (per this report's companion documents), dominates as much or more current research attention as pure algorithmic cryptanalysis.

## 1997 — The AES competition opens

NIST's call for AES candidates was, in retrospect, the single most consequential *institutional* decision of this entire 33-year period, and its consequences are still shaping the field in 2026. The decision to run an **open, international, multi-round competition** — soliciting worldwide submissions, publishing all candidates, inviting public cryptanalysis from the entire global research community, and making the selection criteria and rationale transparent — was not the obvious choice at the time; DES itself had been developed with substantial undisclosed NSA involvement, and there was no guarantee an open process would produce a design as strong. **Why NIST chose openness this time**: DES's approaching obsolescence created an opportunity to do things differently, and the cryptographic research community — by 1997 considerably larger, more international, and more confident than it had been in 1977 — was positioned to make an open competition genuinely credible in a way it might not have been two decades earlier.

## 1998 — DES falls to brute force, not cryptanalysis

The **EFF's "Deep Crack"** machine broke a DES Challenge message via brute-force key search in 56 hours — not a cryptanalytic break at all, just raw, purpose-built hardware finally catching up to the key length. This is a genuinely important, if less glamorous, data point in the field's history: DES's *structure* had survived Matsui's and Biham-Shamir's best attacks reasonably well (both needed more data/computation than brute force for the full cipher); it was the *key length* that killed it operationally, a distinct failure mode from a structural cryptanalytic break, and a lesson (key length must scale with available compute, independent of structural soundness) the field has never needed to relearn since, because it was learned so publicly and so completely here.

## 1999 — Differential Power Analysis and Twofish

**Kocher, Jaffe, and Jun's Differential Power Analysis (DPA)** paper turned power analysis from a plausible-sounding idea into a demonstrated, statistically-powerful attack capable of defeating even implementations with no obviously secret-dependent control flow — the direct ancestor of the entire modern masking/side-channel-countermeasure research program that, by 2026, occupies a full flagship conference (CHES) of its own. In the AES competition, **Twofish** (Schneier, Kelsey, Whiting, Wagner, Hall, Ferguson) and the other four finalists (Serpent, RC6, MARS, alongside eventual winner Rijndael) were narrowed from the fifteen original submissions — the field watching, in close to real time, an unprecedented volume of public cryptanalysis applied to a shared set of serious candidate designs, itself a new kind of research activity (comparative, competitive, openly-published cryptanalysis at scale) that hadn't really existed before this competition.

## 2000 — Rijndael selected

NIST announced **Rijndael** (Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen) as the AES winner, over Serpent (widely regarded as having the largest security margin among finalists but slower), Twofish, RC6 (encumbered by patent concerns), and MARS. The selection rationale — published, transparent, weighing security margin against real hardware/software performance across many platforms — became the template every subsequent NIST cryptographic competition (SHA-3, PQC, Lightweight Cryptography) would explicitly follow. **Why Rijndael specifically**: not because it had the largest margin (Serpent arguably did) but because its combination of margin, elegance, and — critically — the **wide-trail strategy**'s *provable* bound on the best possible differential/linear characteristic probability gave reviewers something Serpent's more empirically-tested margin didn't: a mathematical proof, not just an absence of found attacks, that certain attack classes couldn't do better than a stated bound for the full round count. This is the first time in this history that "we can prove no attack of this class beats X" decisively won out over "we've tried hard and found nothing better than X" — a genuinely new standard of evidence the field would keep raising the bar on for the next two decades (culminating, for ARX specifically, in the long-trail strategy two decades later, Part `04`).

## 2001 — FIPS 197 and the RSA/OAEP proof gap

AES became **FIPS 197**, closing the competition era and opening the deployment era — within a few years it would become, and remain through 2026, the dominant symmetric cipher in the world. Separately, this year brought an important, quieter corrective to the ROM optimism of 1993–1994: growing academic scrutiny of exactly what a ROM proof does and doesn't guarantee, setting up the more formal critique that would arrive in 2004 (Part `02`). Also in this period (the precise dating varies across sources, generally placed 2001–2004 in the literature): **Boneh and Franklin's Identity-Based Encryption from the Weil Pairing**, opening pairing-based cryptography as a distinct, productive research direction that would eventually feed into both practical IBE deployment and, decades later, much of the polynomial-commitment machinery behind zk-SNARK construction (Part `04`–`05` cross-reference).

## What this era established, looking forward

By the end of 2001, the field had: a working, credible template for open, competitive standardization (about to be reused for SHA-3, PQC, and Lightweight Cryptography); a first taste of *provable* security margin via the wide-trail strategy, setting the bar every subsequent primitive's security argument would eventually be measured against; a young but active side-channel research program (DPA, timing attacks) that hadn't yet reshaped mainstream design practice but had planted the seed that eventually would; a still-comfortable, un-urgent relationship with Shor's algorithm; and a symmetric-cryptography landscape (AES) that felt, for the first time in the field's modern history, genuinely, provably solid — a confidence that would be directly and painfully undercut within the next several years, on the *hash function* side specifically, which Part `02` picks up.
