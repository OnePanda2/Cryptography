## 1. Reviewer Perspective on Attack Claims Specifically

### 1.1 What convinces, restated for the attacker's side of the submission

A new-design paper (companion ARX report `08` §3.4's checklist) is convincing when it demonstrates the *absence* of a weakness across a comprehensive evaluation ladder. A new-*attack* paper is convincing on a genuinely inverted standard: it must demonstrate the *presence* of a specific weakness with enough precision that an independent reader could reproduce it exactly. Concretely, per the discovery narratives reconstructed in `04`, a convincing attack paper provides: **(a) the exact, load-bearing assumption being falsified**, stated explicitly rather than left implicit (TinyJambu's paper states directly that the designers' independence assumption is the target); **(b) a formal or experimental complexity figure, not a qualitative claim** ("2^(-62.68)," not "significantly weaker than claimed"); **(c) independent experimental verification wherever computationally feasible** — TinyJambu's discoverers provided actual key/nonce pairs producing colliding tags (`04`), the single strongest form of evidence an attack paper can offer, because it requires no trust in the paper's own complexity analysis at all; and **(d) an honest statement of what the attack does *not* achieve** — a forgery attack paper that doesn't overreach into claiming key recovery it hasn't actually demonstrated is, per this report's synthesis, treated as *more* credible, not less, precisely because overclaiming is the single most common way an attack paper's broader conclusions get later walked back (§2 below).

### 1.2 What immediately weakens credibility

Restating `01` §3's suspicious-versus-unfamiliar distinction from the reviewer's chair specifically: a claimed attack that hasn't been checked against a **negative control** (does the same methodology correctly fail to find a weakness in a deliberately-strengthened variant, or in an established-secure reference design?) is treated with real skepticism, because without that check a reviewer cannot distinguish "this methodology found a genuine weakness" from "this methodology has a bug that makes it find false positives everywhere." Similarly, a complexity claim that hasn't been independently, computationally spot-checked at a small enough scale to verify by brute force is a real, specific red flag — companion ARX report `08` §1.2's replication-and-cross-validation discipline exists precisely because this checking step is where errors most often surface.

### 1.3 How much cryptanalysis is considered sufficient, for a design to be considered adequately vetted

There is no fixed number of published attack papers that constitutes "enough" — the field's actual, working standard, reconstructed from the case studies in `04`, is closer to: **has the design received sustained attention from researchers with genuinely different specializations** (differential/linear specialists, algebraic/SAT specialists, side-channel specialists, and, increasingly, differential-neural specialists) **over a long enough window that the rate of new findings has visibly slowed, not merely that no one has looked recently.** Ascon's clean track record through NIST's multi-year, multi-specialization competition scrutiny (`04`) reads as adequately vetted specifically because the *breadth* of scrutiny, not just its duration, was substantial; a design that's received years of attention from only one narrow specialization is, by this standard, less thoroughly vetted than its calendar age alone would suggest.

### 1.4 When a design is rejected despite appearing secure

Directly restating and extending companion design-process report `05`'s Ascon-over-Sparkle and Xoodyak's non-selection case studies from the cryptanalyst's rather than the designer's vantage point: a design can clear every technical bar this report and its companions describe and still not be the field's chosen standard, because standardization committees (per companion history report `08` §1.1) weigh implementation maturity, footprint, and API flexibility alongside pure cryptanalytic cleanliness — a cryptanalyst's job is to correctly, honestly report "I found nothing" when that's true, and resist any pressure (self-imposed or otherwise) to manufacture a finding just because a design is otherwise being deprioritized for non-cryptanalytic reasons.

## 2. Failed Research — Cryptanalytic Conclusions That Didn't Survive

### 2.1 The XSL attack against AES

Covered in full in companion history report `07` §1.1; the discovery-process-specific lesson worth restating here, from the *reviewing* rather than the *designing* side: the XSL attack's initial reception was, by several contemporary accounts, more excited than the eventual, carefully-checked verdict warranted — and the correction came specifically from independent researchers doing exactly `04`'s TinyJambu-style work: checking whether the paper's own complexity analysis actually held at real AES parameters, rather than accepting the asymptotic claim on the strength of its cleverness. **The lesson for a working cryptanalyst, stated directly**: your own paper's most exciting-sounding claim is exactly the one you should be most rigorous about independently verifying before publication, because it's the one the rest of the field will scrutinize hardest and fastest.

### 2.2 The related-key AES-256 attacks — a correct result whose *significance*, not its mathematics, was contested

Biryukov and Khovratovich's 2009 related-key attacks against AES-256 are mathematically sound and remain, to this day, a genuine, real academic result — but their *practical* significance was, and remains, a live subject of debate within the field, because the threat model they require (an attacker who can obtain ciphertexts encrypted under keys related by a known or chosen mathematical relationship) is one many practitioners consider unrealistic for how AES-256 is actually deployed in real protocols, which almost never expose related-key relationships to an adversary. **Why this belongs in a "failed research" file even though the mathematics is entirely sound**: it's a clean, well-documented illustration of a distinct, common failure mode — not an error in the attack itself, but a mismatch between a technically correct result and the threat model needed to make it *practically* significant, and reviewers and practitioners alike, per companion design-process report `01` §4's explicit threat-model-scoping discipline, are trained to ask "under what threat model does this matter" as a distinct question from "is this mathematically correct," precisely because conflating the two — treating any technically-valid attack as automatically practically alarming — is a real, recurring misreading pattern in how cryptanalytic results get reported and understood outside the specialist community that produced them.

### 2.3 Overclaiming distinguisher significance, generally — the pattern Keccak's own team explicitly guards against

Restating `04`'s Keccak case study from this file's specific angle: the field's now-standard discipline of explicitly, separately stating "a real structural anomaly exists" and "this anomaly constitutes a break of the actual security claim" exists precisely *because* the field has, historically, seen distinguisher results reported (sometimes by the discoverers, more often by secondary coverage) in ways that blurred this distinction — a pattern this report names generally, without needing to single out a specific instance, because the corrective institutional practice (the Keccak team's own public, explicit third-party-results cataloging, `04`) is itself the clearest evidence the field recognized this as a recurring problem worth building a standing defense against.

### 2.4 Weak metrics and incomplete evaluations, as a recurring publication-bias-adjacent pattern

Restating companion ARX report `08` §2's named mistakes from the cryptanalysis-paper side specifically (as distinct from the design-paper side that report's file focuses on): a cryptanalysis paper that reports a positive finding (an attack that works) is, structurally, more publishable than one reporting a negative finding (a thorough evaluation that found nothing) — a real, field-wide publication-bias dynamic that, per this report's synthesis, is worth naming explicitly as a distinct pressure independent of any individual researcher's rigor, because it means the *absence* of a published attack against a given design is weaker evidence of that design's security than a naive reading of "no one has published a break" would suggest — precisely why §1.3 above frames adequate vetting around sustained, broad *scrutiny*, not merely the absence of published breaks, which could equally reflect insufficient attention as genuine security.

## 3. Common Misconceptions, Stated Directly

Synthesizing across this entire report, the specific misconceptions worth naming plainly as a closing reference:

1. **"A distinguisher against the full round count is the same thing as a break."** Directly false, per Keccak's zero-sum lineage (`04`) — the gap between the two is not a technicality, it's the entire substance of the security argument.
2. **"An attack requiring an unrealistic threat model (related keys, chosen ciphertext at implausible volume) is not worth taking seriously."** Also not quite right — per §2.2, such results remain genuine, valid data points about a design's *margin*, even when their direct practical significance is properly contested; the mistake is conflating "not immediately practically alarming" with "not real."
3. **"If a design's own analysis found comfortable margin, third-party scrutiny is a formality."** Directly contradicted by TinyJambu (`04`) — a design team's own analysis can carry an unexamined assumption invisible to them precisely because they built the analysis around it.
4. **"A cryptanalysis paper's absence of a follow-up break means the design has been fully vetted."** Contradicted by §2.4's publication-bias discussion — absence of a published break is weaker evidence than it's often treated as, especially for designs that haven't received broad, sustained, multi-specialization attention.
5. **"A countermeasure that closed one attack closes the whole attack surface."** Directly contradicted by Rocca's multi-round arms race (`04`) — a fix targeted at a specific, identified weakness leaves every *other* assumption the design makes exactly as untested as before.
6. **"Passing statistical randomness tests is meaningful cryptographic evidence."** Covered in full in companion ARX report `06` §2.2 and restated in `03`'s "incomplete avalanche" entry — worth repeating here because it's specifically a cryptanalyst-facing misconception as much as a designer-facing one: a cryptanalyst who runs only statistical tests against a candidate weakness and reports a clean result has not actually cleared the design, per the exact same logic `02` Stage 4 states from the discovery-workflow side.
