# 04 — Historical Case Studies: How the Weakness Was Actually Found

*Part of a multi-file report — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map. Assumes `01`–`03`. Attack mechanics for the well-established cases (DES, RC4, MD5/SHA-1, Speck/Simon) are documented in full in this conversation's companion reports; this file's distinct contribution is the *discovery process* reconstruction, and it leans especially hard on genuinely new grounding for Keccak, Rocca, and TinyJambu.*

---

## DES — differential cryptanalysis, discovered twice

**What was initially believed**: DES's S-boxes, released with no public design rationale, were widely assumed adequate largely on the strength of NSA's involvement and the absence of any public break for over a decade. **How the weakness was actually found, the first time**: Biham and Shamir's 1990 differential cryptanalysis wasn't originally aimed at DES specifically — it emerged from a broader research program systematically asking, for block ciphers generally, "does a fixed input difference propagate to a fixed output difference with probability meaningfully above 2^(-n)?" (`01` §1's independence-claim framing, applied retroactively: the implicit claim any block cipher's design makes is that no such biased propagation exists at a consequential probability). Applying this general question specifically to DES's published S-boxes was the actual discovery step — not a DES-specific insight, but a general technique whose first serious target happened to be the most important cipher available to test it against. **Why it had been overlooked for over a decade**: the technique itself (systematically tracking difference propagation) hadn't been formalized and published yet — this is a case where the "warning sign" (`03`) existed in the S-box tables the whole time, visible to anyone who thought to compute a full differential distribution table, but no one had yet built the systematic methodology to know what to look for or how to chain single-round biases into a full-cipher attack. **The second discovery, more consequential for how the field understands overlooked weaknesses**: Don Coppersmith's 1994 revelation that IBM's original DES design team had known about differential cryptanalysis in the 1970s and had specifically hardened the S-boxes against it — meaning the "warning sign" hadn't actually been overlooked by the original designers at all, only by the *public* research community, a genuinely important, still-cited data point about the historical gap between classified and open cryptanalytic knowledge (companion history report `01`).

## RC4 — the slow accumulation of small, individually-dismissable biases

**What was initially believed**: RC4's simplicity and over a decade of practical use (WEP, early TLS) without a full break created a strong, if never fully rigorous, presumption of adequacy. **How the weakness was actually found**: not as a single discovery but as a genuinely slow accumulation — Fluhrer, Mantin, and Shamir's 2001 key-scheduling-algorithm weakness (exploited devastatingly against WEP specifically, where key material was predictably related to a public IV) was followed, over the subsequent decade-plus, by a steady sequence of increasingly refined statistical bias findings in RC4's keystream output itself, each individually modest but cumulatively serious enough that by the mid-2010s (the "RC4 NOMORE" attack) the biases were practically exploitable against TLS specifically. **Why this pattern is instructive for the discovery-process question specifically**: RC4 is this report's clearest case of `01` §5's fifth pattern — "this statistical bias is too small to matter" being individually true at each point in the sequence and cumulatively false — and it illustrates a genuine, recurring methodological lesson: a cryptanalyst who finds a small bias and dismisses it because it's individually unexploitable is asking the wrong question; the right question is whether the bias is *structural* (arising from the algorithm's fixed design, not from a specific weak key) and therefore certain to still be there, waiting to be combined with the next small bias someone else finds.

## MD5 and SHA-1 — targeted differential cryptanalysis of a hash function's compression function

**What was initially believed**: hash functions were, through the 1990s, assumed collision-resistant largely on the absence of a found collision, without the kind of systematic, chained-difference methodology DES had already received (companion history report `02`). **How the weakness was actually found**: Wang, Feng, Lai, and Yu's 2004 MD5 break, and the subsequent 2005 SHA-1 theoretical break, applied differential cryptanalysis's core methodology — but adapted specifically to the very different target a compression function represents relative to a block cipher (no secret key to recover; the goal is finding *any* two inputs producing the same output, not recovering a specific secret). The actual discovery insight, reconstructed from the technique's own structure: rather than chasing a single, high-probability full differential path (as DES-style cryptanalysis does), the Wang-et-al. methodology chains many *local*, high-probability, carefully-controlled differential conditions across the compression function's rounds, using message-modification techniques to actively steer intermediate values into satisfying those conditions rather than merely hoping a random input satisfies them — a genuinely different, more constructive attack style than classical block-cipher differential cryptanalysis, developed specifically because the hash-function setting (attacker fully controls both inputs) makes this constructive approach available in a way a block-cipher attacker, without key knowledge, doesn't have.

## Keccak — the distinguisher that never became a break, and why that distinction is the whole lesson

**What was initially believed**: as the SHA-3 competition's structurally-different, deliberately-scrutinized alternative to Merkle–Damgård (companion history report `02`–`03`), Keccak received an unusually large, unusually early volume of dedicated third-party cryptanalysis — the Keccak team itself maintained (and maintains) a public page cataloging every third-party result, an explicit, institutional invitation for exactly this kind of scrutiny. **How the actual findings unfolded, reconstructed as a discovery sequence**: Aumasson and Meier's 2009 zero-sum distinguishers were the opening move — applying Lai's 1994 higher-order-derivative technique (the same mathematical root as cube attacks and integral attacks, `03`'s "low algebraic degree" entry) specifically to Keccak's permutation, computing sets of inputs whose outputs, summed, provably equal zero for a structured reason tied to the permutation's algebraic degree — and finding this distinguisher works, remarkably, **against the full 24-round permutation**, not just a reduced-round variant. Duan and Lai's 2011 follow-up improved the distinguisher's parameters further, still at full round count. **Why this alarming-sounding result never became a break, and how the field learned to say so precisely**: the distinguisher exploits a genuine, real algebraic property of the *bare permutation* — but the sponge construction's actual security argument (companion ARX report `05` §3.2–3.3's indifferentiability framework) doesn't require the underlying permutation to be indistinguishable from random under *every conceivable* distinguishing game, only under the specific ones that matter for the sponge's own security reduction, and the zero-sum distinguisher's specific structure (a very particular, structured input set, not something an attacker controlling a normal hash-function query interface could naturally produce) doesn't translate into an attack against the actual construction. **The subsequent, genuinely more consequential thread**: cube attacks and "conditional cube testers" (Dinur et al.'s EUROCRYPT 2015 work, refined by Huang, Wang, Xu, Wang, Zhao, and further by Li et al. at ToSC 2019) targeted **keyed** modes specifically — Keccak-MAC, Keyak — where, per `03`'s "weak permutations" and `01` §2's fourth warning-sign, an attacker's ability to make chosen queries under a fixed secret key gives real leverage a plain hash-function security case was never built to withstand, and these attacks achieved genuine, practical-complexity key recovery against reduced-round variants of the *keyed* constructions specifically — a sharp, concrete illustration that "Keccak is secure as SHA-3" and "an arbitrary keyed construction built from the Keccak permutation is secure" are different claims requiring different, independently-conducted analysis, exactly the lesson `03`'s "weak permutations" entry states in the abstract. **What lessons survived**: the discipline of clearly distinguishing "a real, verified structural anomaly exists" from "this anomaly constitutes an attack against the actual security claim" — visible directly in how the Keccak team's own public cataloging of third-party results consistently and explicitly makes this distinction for every listed finding, rather than treating every distinguisher as equally alarming.

## ChaCha and Salsa20 — the slow, healthy, still-ongoing version of this process

Covered in full technical detail across companion ARX report `04` §2.3 and companion history report `04`–`05`; the discovery-process-specific point worth adding here: this is this report's example of the funnel in `02` operating exactly as designed, over nearly two decades, without ever reaching a break — Beierle, Leander, and Todo's 2020 differential-linear refinement, Coutinho and Peyrin's 2023 MILP-boosted extension, Xu, Xu, Tan, and Qi's 2024 further push, and Flórez-Gutiérrez and Todo's 2025 bit-puncturing technique are each, individually, a cryptanalyst (or team) taking the existing best-known distinguisher, per `02`'s Stage 5–6 escalation, and asking specifically "can the Probabilistic Neutral Bits selection, or the differential half of the chain, be tightened further using a *newer* piece of the automated-search toolkit than the previous paper had available" — a discovery process driven almost entirely by *tooling* improvement (each new MILP/SAT technique applied to the same well-studied target) rather than by any single flash of new structural insight into ChaCha itself, worth contrasting directly against Keccak's more structurally-driven discovery sequence above.

## Ascon — the case study where third-party scrutiny mostly confirmed, rather than found weakness

Per companion ARX report `02` §2.7 and `08` §3.1, Ascon's NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition trajectory involved substantial third-party cryptanalytic scrutiny (differential/linear/cube-attack evaluation against reduced-round variants, standard practice for any live competition candidate per `03`'s full checklist applied systematically) without ever producing a finding threatening the standardized parameter sets — worth including specifically as a contrast case to the others in this file: **not every serious, sustained cryptanalytic effort finds a weakness**, and Ascon's clean track record through a multi-year, heavily-scrutinized competition process is itself a genuine, positive data point about the design, not merely an absence of a story to tell.

## Rocca and Rocca-S — a genuine, multi-round arms race, still unfolding as this report is written

**What was initially believed**: Rocca (Sakamoto, Liu, Nakano, Kiyomoto, Isobe, 2022), an AES-round-function-based, extremely high-throughput AEAD cipher designed for beyond-5G/6G bandwidth requirements, built on a design lineage (AEGIS, Tiaoxin-346) that had previously shown a specific weakness pattern — Minaud's 2014 work had found that some earlier AES-round-based designs using only *simple quadratic Boolean combining functions* between AES-round applications leaked exploitable statistical bias. Rocca's designers responded to this known pattern directly, using a **full AES round** (not a simpler quadratic function) as the combining/mixing step specifically to close that avenue — a textbook instance of `01` §2's "countermeasure targeted at a known, specific prior weakness" design move. **How the next weakness was actually found**: Hosoyamada, Isobe, Sasaki, and a fourth collaborator's 2022 "Cryptanalysis of Rocca and feasibility of its security claim" paper (ToSC 2022) didn't target the AES-round-combining-function pattern Rocca had already defended against — per `01` §2's fifth warning sign, they instead examined precisely what Rocca's *stated* security claims actually required versus what its formal argument established, finding a genuine key-recovery-relevant gap, which directly motivated the revised **Rocca-S** (with an enlarged 256-bit key and tag). **The pattern continuing into a third round**: a follow-up paper ("Key Recovery, Universal Forgery, and Committing Attacks against Revised Rocca") examined specifically how Rocca-S's **finalization phase** — the step converting internal state into the final authentication tag — affects security, finding further attacks specific to that phase; separately, Derbez, Fouque, Isobe, Rahman, and Schrottenloher's 2024 "Key committing attacks against AES-based AEAD schemes" paper identified an entirely different attack *category* (key-committing attacks — can an attacker find a ciphertext that decrypts validly under two different keys, a property relevant to certain protocol-composition security goals rather than confidentiality/integrity directly) affecting Rocca, Rocca-S, and the whole AES-round-based AEAD family (AEGIS, Tiaoxin) simultaneously, precisely because they share the underlying design pattern. **The most recent, and most directly instructive, turn in this arms race**: a 2026 analysis of **HiAE**, a different, later cipher that adopted Rocca's key-dependent-initialization countermeasure (introduced specifically to close the earlier key-recovery gap), found that countermeasure insufficient in HiAE's specific context — precisely `01` §2's second warning sign playing out in real time, with a dated, traceable example: a fix validated in the context it was designed for did not automatically transfer when reused in a structurally-related but distinct design. **What this entire sequence teaches about discovery process specifically**: each stage of this arms race was found not by inventing a new attack technique, but by a cryptanalyst asking, freshly, "what does *this specific* design's *current* security argument actually require to be true" — the same question, re-asked against a moving target, rather than a single technique applied once and exhausted.

## Simon and Speck — the discovery process shaped by provenance, not just mathematics

Attack mechanics and the full cryptanalytic record are covered in companion ARX report `02` §2.9 and `04`; the discovery-process point specific to this pair, not fully drawn out there: because Simon and Speck were released by the NSA in the immediate aftermath of the Dual_EC_DRBG/Snowden reckoning (companion history report `03`), the *volume and intensity* of third-party cryptanalytic attention they received was measurably higher, and arrived faster, than a comparable design from a less institutionally-scrutinized source likely would have — a real, if hard-to-fully-quantify, illustration that `01` §3's "unfamiliar versus suspicious" distinction can, in practice, get blurred by an institution's trust history: Simon/Speck's provenance made the entire research community's collective prior lean toward "suspicious" rather than merely "unfamiliar," concentrating attention that ultimately found no full break but did, per companion history report `04`, directly cost the ciphers formal ISO standardization on trust grounds regardless.

## TinyJambu — the sharpest available example of "which assumption to question"

**What was initially believed**: TinyJambu's own designers, in their NIST Lightweight Cryptography submission, evaluated security using a standard, expected methodology — MILP-based counting of the minimum number of "active" AND gates (the single nonlinear component per round in TinyJambu's NLFSR-based design) required for any differential trail, directly analogous to the wide-trail-strategy active-S-box counting this entire report's companion documents trace back to AES (companion history report `01`, `06`). This produced a stated forgery-attack probability bound of 2^(-80), treated by the designers as adequate security margin. **How the weakness was actually found, reconstructed directly from the discoverers' own stated contribution**: Saha, Sasaki, Shi, Sibleyras, Sun, and Zhang's paper (the first third-party security analysis of TinyJambu, per the paper's own framing) identified precisely the gap `01` §2's second warning sign describes in the abstract: the designers' active-AND-gate counting method **assumed the active AND gates behave independently**, but TinyJambu's specific NLFSR structure produces a **first-order correlation between multiple active AND gates that occurs frequently** — a structural fact about the specific design, not a general flaw in active-component counting as a methodology. Building a refined model that correctly accounts for this correlation (rather than assuming independence), the discoverers found a 338-round differential trail with probability 2^(-62.68) — dramatically better, from an attacker's perspective, than the designers' own 2^(-80) bound, and enough to mount a genuine forgery attack breaking the claimed 64-bit security level. **The direct, concrete institutional consequence**: NIST permitted a tweak, increasing TinyJambu's round count from 384 to 640 specifically in response — a rare, clean case where a discovery process this report can fully reconstruct (question the independence assumption → build a correlation-aware model → find a materially better trail → the standardization body responds with a concrete parameter change) produced a directly traceable institutional outcome, with no ambiguity about cause and effect. **The story continues**: a further 2022 paper found practical **related-key** forgery attacks against the tweaked TinyJambu-192/256 variants specifically — the original designers' and Saha-et-al.'s both analyses had been conducted in the single-key setting; the related-key setting, per `03`'s "weak key schedules" entry's recursive-checklist point, turned out to admit a high-probability differential characteristic when differences were introduced specifically into the key state rather than the message/nonce state, extending all the way to full, practical key-recovery (not just forgery) for all three key sizes. **What this entire case study teaches, stated as directly as possible**: the single most replicable, most teachable discovery-process lesson in this entire report is contained in this one case — *find the specific simplifying assumption a design's own security proof depends on, and check, specifically and directly, whether that exact assumption is actually true for this exact design* — not a general attack technique, but a disciplined, targeted question, asked precisely.
