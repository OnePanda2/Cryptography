## 1. ARX Cryptography — History and Structural Identity

### 1.1 Origins

ARX (Addition-Rotation-XOR) is not a single design but a **design paradigm**: build every nonlinear and linear operation in a cryptographic primitive from exactly three instructions — addition modulo 2^n, bitwise rotation, and XOR — and nothing else (no S-box lookup tables, no field multiplication). The paradigm's modern lineage traces most directly to **Ron Rivest's MD4/MD5** hash functions (1990–1992) and **RC5** (1994), which used modular addition and data-dependent rotation as their primary nonlinear engines, but the paradigm's *deliberate, principled* articulation — choosing ARX specifically for its constant-time and portability properties, rather than incidentally happening to use addition and rotation — is most associated with **Daniel J. Bernstein's Salsa20** (2005, submitted to eSTREAM) and its refinement **ChaCha** (2008). Bernstein's own public design rationale for Salsa20 explicitly frames the choice as a response to the software-timing-side-channel weaknesses of table-lookup-based AES implementations (a live, practically-demonstrated concern in the mid-2000s, before AES-NI hardware existed) — ARX ciphers have **no data-dependent memory access pattern at all**, so there is structurally nothing for a cache-timing attack to observe.

### 1.2 Why ARX became important

Three converging pressures, each independently sufficient to explain ARX's rise, together explain why it became a dominant paradigm for an entire class of designs rather than a curiosity:

1. **Side-channel resistance by construction.** Table-lookup-free execution eliminates an entire attack class (cache-timing) that plagued naive software AES for over a decade before hardware acceleration became universal. This mattered enormously on platforms without dedicated crypto silicon — mobile chipsets historically, embedded microcontrollers, and any environment where a defender can't assume AES-NI-equivalent hardware exists.
2. **Software performance without special hardware.** Addition, rotation, and XOR are among the cheapest instructions any CPU has, executed with single-cycle or near-single-cycle throughput on essentially every architecture built since the 1970s, and — critically — they vectorize extremely well under SIMD (a rotation and an XOR applied to 4 or 8 independent lanes simultaneously is exactly what AVX2/AVX-512/NEON are good at). This gives ARX designs consistently excellent cycles-per-byte figures on general-purpose CPUs, competitive with or exceeding hardware-accelerated AES on many platforms.
3. **Design and implementation simplicity.** No S-box means no table to select, no S-box-construction cryptanalytic literature to internalize, and — for hardware implementers — potentially smaller gate counts for a given security target in specific contexts (though this is workload- and target-dependent; see 1.5 below).

### 1.3 Advantages

- Constant-time by construction (no secret-dependent memory access).
- Strong, portable software performance without requiring dedicated hardware.
- Small, simple reference implementations — often genuinely fitting on a single page, which aids auditability (a real, non-decorative security property: a smaller trusted-computing-base is easier to review exhaustively, and WireGuard's design philosophy, covered in this conversation's earlier general-cryptography report, makes exactly this argument explicitly).
- SIMD-friendly by nature — the same three operations parallelize near-trivially across lanes, which is part of why BLAKE3's tree-parallel design (Part 2 of this file, `02-...`) achieves such large multi-core/SIMD speedups relative to BLAKE2.
- No S-box design/selection burden — designers don't need the differential-uniformity/nonlinearity optimization work AES's S-box required; the "S-box" (in ARX's ARX-box formulations like Alzette, see `02`) is instead a short fixed sequence of add/rotate/XOR/constant-injection steps whose properties are *derived*, not hand-optimized bit by bit the way a genuine substitution table is.

### 1.4 Weaknesses

- **Modular addition's nonlinearity is comparatively "thin" and unevenly distributed across bit positions** — the differential and linear probability of addition is *not* uniform across output bit positions (low-order bits carry less accumulated carry-chain influence than high-order bits), which creates exploitable structure that a well-designed S-box's flatter differential/linear distribution avoids. This is the single most important mathematical fact separating ARX cryptanalysis from SPN cryptanalysis and is covered in full bit-level detail in Part 2 below.
- **Harder to give clean, simple provable security bounds** relative to AES's wide-trail strategy, precisely because addition's differential/linear behavior depends on carry propagation in a way that resists the same clean "count active S-boxes" counting argument the wide-trail strategy relies on for SPN designs — this is *why* the "long-trail strategy" (Part `03`) required real, separate research effort to develop rather than being a direct translation of the SPN result.
- **Rotation constant and round-count choice is comparatively under-constrained by simple design rules** relative to an SPN's S-box-table-driven design process — a poor rotation-constant/schedule choice can leave exploitable low-diffusion patterns that only automated search reliably catches (Part `04`), meaning ARX design without serious automated-tooling verification is considerably riskier than SPN design without it.
- **No hardware-acceleration analogue to AES-NI exists for any single ARX design** — because ARX ciphers vary in their exact operation sequence (unlike the fixed-instruction AES round that AES-NI hard-wires), there's no equivalent silicon fast path, meaning ARX's performance advantage is specifically a *software-without-dedicated-hardware* advantage, not an absolute one; on a platform with genuine AES-NI, hardware AES-GCM typically outperforms software ChaCha20-Poly1305.

### 1.5 Comparison with other structural paradigms

| Paradigm | Nonlinearity source | Diffusion mechanism | Side-channel posture (software) | Canonical example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SPN** | Fixed S-box substitution | Linear mixing layer (matrix multiplication) | Table-lookup S-box vulnerable to cache-timing unless hardware-accelerated or bitsliced | AES |
| **Feistel** | Round function F (often itself SPN-like or ARX-like internally) | Alternating half-swap | Depends entirely on F's internals | DES, Camellia |
| **ARX** | Modular addition's carry chain | Rotation + XOR | Constant-time by construction | Salsa20/ChaCha, Speck |
| **Sponge/permutation** | Depends on the permutation's internal structure (can itself be ARX, as in Xoodoo/Ascon-permutation-adjacent designs, or SPN-like, as in Keccak/Ascon) | Absorb/squeeze over a large state | Depends on the permutation's internals | Keccak (SPN-style permutation), Xoodoo (ARX-adjacent, technically AND-based, see `02`) |

A crucial, frequently-conflated point worth stating precisely: **"sponge" and "ARX" are orthogonal design-axis choices, not competing paradigms** — sponge/duplex describes how a *permutation* is used to build a hash function or AEAD scheme (Part 1, general cryptography report, §4.2 of this conversation's earlier deliverable), while ARX describes what the *permutation itself* is built from internally. Xoodoo (the permutation inside Xoodyak) is a sponge construction whose internal permutation uses XOR, rotation, and AND (not addition) — making it "AND-RX" rather than strictly ARX, an important, precise distinction covered further in `02`. Ascon's permutation is likewise a sponge-style construction, but its internal round function is a genuine 5-bit SPN-style S-box layer plus a linear diffusion layer — **Ascon is not an ARX design at all**, despite frequently appearing in ARX-adjacent discussions because of its lightweight-cryptography competition overlap with genuinely ARX-based competitors like Sparkle. Getting this distinction exactly right is a basic literacy check for anyone entering ARX research, because muddling it is a fast way to misattribute a cryptanalytic result to the wrong design family.

### 1.6 Hardware implications

ARX designs generally implement efficiently in hardware (small combinational logic per round for narrow word sizes), but do **not** benefit from the same dedicated-instruction acceleration AES enjoys via AES-NI/ARMv8 Crypto Extensions — there is no "ChaCha-NI." Where ARX genuinely wins in hardware-constrained contexts is **lightweight/IoT silicon**, where the absence of a large S-box lookup table (which costs real gate area in a hardware S-box implementation, or real ROM/RAM in a software lookup-table implementation on a microcontroller) can translate into a smaller footprint — this was a live, explicitly-evaluated consideration in the NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition, where both Ascon (SPN-sponge) and Sparkle (ARX) were finalists precisely because both offer genuinely small hardware/software footprints via different structural routes, and NIST's own finalist-comparison documentation (per this report's search record) evaluates them head-to-head on exactly these axes.

### 1.7 Software, portability, and SIMD implications

This is ARX's strongest, most consistently-cited practical advantage. Because addition, rotation, and XOR are universal instructions present (in some form) on every CPU architecture ever built, an ARX design's reference C implementation typically runs correctly and reasonably fast *everywhere* without architecture-specific code paths — a genuine portability property AES-in-software historically lacked (before AES-NI, portable AES implementations were meaningfully slower and, per Part `01`'s discussion of the general cryptography report, side-channel-risky). SIMD vectorization compounds this: ChaCha20's quarter-round structure is explicitly designed so that **4 independent quarter-rounds can be computed in parallel across SIMD lanes**, which is exactly why AVX2/AVX-512/NEON implementations of ChaCha20 achieve such strong throughput — the algorithm's internal parallelism structure was a first-class design consideration, not an implementation afterthought (a design/implementation co-design lesson worth internalizing for anyone designing a new ARX permutation: **SIMD-friendliness should be a Part `03`-stage design decision, not something left to implementers to discover is hard later**).

### 1.8 Modern relevance and future outlook

ARX remains the dominant paradigm for software-oriented, side-channel-conscious symmetric design as of 2026: ChaCha20-Poly1305 is a default TLS 1.3 ciphersuite, BLAKE3 is increasingly the pragmatic choice for high-throughput non-standards-mandated hashing, Speck/Simon (NSA-designed, 2013) remain heavily studied lightweight-cryptography reference points despite NIST ultimately standardizing Ascon rather than an ARX design for its lightweight-crypto standard, and the entire Sparkle/Alzette/long-trail-strategy research program (CRYPTO 2020 onward) represents active, current, provable-security-focused ARX design research, not a closed chapter. The field's live frontier — covered throughout this report, especially `04` and `09` — is squarely in **automated cryptanalysis tooling** (MILP/SAT modeling of addition specifically) and, increasingly, **differential-neural cryptanalysis**, both of which are measurably advancing the best-known attacks against canonical ARX targets (Speck, Simon, ChaCha) year over year as of 2023–2025, meaning "designing a new ARX permutation responsibly" in 2026 requires fluency with tooling that didn't exist, or existed in a meaningfully weaker form, five years ago.

## 2. ARX Building Blocks — The Bit-Level Mathematics

### 2.1 Why exactly these three operations

**Addition modulo 2^n** is ARX's *only* source of nonlinearity — XOR is linear over GF(2)^n by definition, and rotation is a linear (in fact, a fixed permutation) operation on bit positions. Everything about ARX cryptanalysis, ARX diffusion, and ARX security-margin design ultimately reduces to **understanding modular addition's nonlinear behavior precisely**, because it's the only place nonlinearity can possibly come from. This is worth stating as starkly as possible because it's the single organizing fact of the entire ARX research literature: an ARX permutation with n additions per round has, in a meaningful sense, exactly n "S-boxes" per round, each of which is a modular adder rather than a lookup table, and every diffusion/cryptanalysis question in this report ultimately traces back to that adder's exact bit-level behavior.

### 2.2 Carry propagation and why it creates nonlinearity

For two n-bit words x and y, addition mod 2^n is computed bit-by-bit from the least-significant bit with a **carry chain**: bit i of the sum depends on bits 0 through i of both x and y (via the propagating carry), not just on bit i of x and y individually. This carry dependency is exactly what makes addition nonlinear over GF(2)^n (if it were carry-free, addition mod 2^n would just be XOR). The practical, cryptanalytically-crucial consequence: **the differential and linear probability of addition is a function of bit position** — low-order bits have shorter carry histories and behave "more linearly" (addition and XOR agree more often at low bit positions for random inputs) than high-order bits, which accumulate more carry-chain influence. This positional non-uniformity is precisely what the Lipmaa–Moriai algorithm (2001) formalizes: an efficient, exact method for computing the differential probability of addition given input and output XOR-differences, expressed via bit-level conditions on where the differences agree/disagree relative to a computed carry-difference — the single most cited, most foundational tool in the entire ARX differential-cryptanalysis literature, and the direct mathematical ancestor of every MILP/SAT addition model covered in `05`.

### 2.3 Rotation

Bit rotation (circular shift) by a fixed amount is a linear, bijective operation — it permutes bit positions without altering their values, contributing **zero nonlinearity** and, on its own, **zero diffusion across bit-position classes it doesn't visit** (a rotation alone just relabels positions; it doesn't mix values *between* different data words). Rotation's actual cryptographic job in an ARX round is to **reposition bits so that a subsequent addition's carry chain touches bit positions it wouldn't otherwise reach**, spreading the nonlinear addition's local, carry-chain-limited influence across the full word width over multiple rounds — this is the mechanistic reason ARX designs alternate addition and rotation rather than, say, doing all additions first and all rotations after: **the rotation is what turns addition's inherently local (low-bit-to-high-bit, one-directional) carry propagation into eventual full-word diffusion**, and choosing rotation *amounts* that achieve this quickly, with few rounds, is exactly the rotation-constant-selection problem covered in `04`.

### 2.4 XOR

XOR is linear, bijective, and self-inverse, contributing zero nonlinearity but serving three essential structural roles: (1) **combining two data words or a data word and a round constant/key material** without any nonlinear interaction (the linear "mixing" step complementing addition's nonlinear step, in the Shannon confusion/diffusion sense — Part 1 of this conversation's general-cryptography report — addition delivers confusion, rotation+XOR jointly deliver diffusion); (2) **injecting round constants** to break the otherwise-present rotational symmetry a pure add-rotate structure would have (addressed directly in `04`'s rotational-cryptanalysis discussion — round constants are specifically chosen to *not* be rotation-invariant, defeating rotational and RX-cryptanalysis's exploitation of that symmetry, per Ashur & Liu's original RX-cryptanalysis framing); and (3) **key/nonce/counter injection points**, which double as the primary lever designers have for tuning a stream cipher or PRNG's forward-secrecy and state-update properties (Part `06`).

### 2.5 Nonlinearity, formalized

The **differential probability** of addition, DP⁺(α, β → γ) — the probability that inputs with XOR-difference α, β produce outputs with XOR-difference γ, over uniformly random inputs — is computed exactly, in linear time in the word size, by the Lipmaa–Moriai algorithm, via a bit-level automaton tracking whether each bit position is a "carry-generating," "carry-propagating," or "carry-absorbing" position given α, β, γ. This exact-probability-in-linear-time property is *why* MILP/SAT modeling of addition (`05`) is tractable at all — without it, exhaustively computing DP⁺ for every possible (α, β, γ) triple would be infeasible for any real word size (2^96 triples for 32-bit words). The analogous **linear correlation** of addition (the bias of a linear approximation through the adder) has a comparable, if somewhat more involved, closed-form characterization developed in the years following Lipmaa–Moriai, used identically as the mathematical foundation for automated linear-trail search.

### 2.6 Rotation constants and rotation schedules

A **rotation constant** is the fixed number of bit positions a given rotation operation shifts by; a **rotation schedule** is the full sequence of rotation constants used across a design's quarter-rounds/rounds (ChaCha's schedule — 16, 12, 8, 7 for its four quarter-round rotations — is the canonical example every ARX researcher should be able to recite and explain the *reasoning* behind, not just memorize). Good rotation constants share several empirically- and analytically-motivated properties, covered in full search-methodology detail in `04`: they should avoid small common factors with the word size that would create short-period, low-diffusion cycles; they should be chosen so that, in combination with the design's addition/XOR placement, the *number of rounds needed to achieve full diffusion* (every output bit depends on every input bit) is minimized; and — critically, per the RX-cryptanalysis literature — they interact with round-constant placement to determine whether the design has any *exploitable rotational symmetry* at all.

### 2.7 Diffusion mechanisms and state mixing

Diffusion in an ARX design is an emergent property of the addition/rotation/XOR sequence and lane-interconnection structure (which data words feed into which additions across a round), not of any single operation. **Lane interaction** — how a permutation's multiple parallel data words (ChaCha's 16 32-bit words arranged in a 4×4 grid; Speck's two n-bit words) are cross-connected between rounds — is the design-level lever controlling how quickly local, single-addition nonlinearity and diffusion spread across the *entire* state, not just within one word. ChaCha's alternating "column round" and "diagonal round" structure (Part `02` covers this in full structural detail) is a specific, deliberately-engineered lane-interaction pattern chosen precisely to achieve fast full-state diffusion by mixing both "straight" (column) and "crossed" (diagonal) word groupings across successive rounds — a design pattern directly analogous in *purpose*, though not mechanism, to AES's ShiftRows+MixColumns achieving full diffusion in exactly two rounds (Part 2, general cryptography report).

### 2.8 Word sizes — 32-bit, 64-bit, 128-bit tradeoffs

| Word size | Representative designs | Tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| 32-bit | ChaCha, Salsa20, Speck32/64 (toy/research variant), Simon32/64 | Matches native register width on essentially every 32-bit-and-up CPU; smallest addition carry chains (fastest per-operation latency); most-studied size in the automated-cryptanalysis literature (most MILP/SAT tooling papers benchmark on 32-bit-word designs specifically because full-round verification is more computationally tractable) |
| 64-bit | BLAKE2b, SipHash, Speck128/128 (uses two 64-bit words), Threefry | Matches native 64-bit CPU register width (no need to synthesize wider arithmetic from narrower registers); longer carry chains than 32-bit (more room for addition's nonlinearity to accumulate per operation, at the cost of needing correspondingly more rounds' worth of automated-search verification to confirm the accumulated margin is real, not merely "more bits, presumably safer") |
| 128-bit (per lane) | Some AVX-512/wide-SIMD-oriented experimental designs; lane width in wide-permutation constructions | Requires either native 128-bit arithmetic (rare on general CPUs outside specific SIMD-lane semantics) or software-emulated double-word addition (a carry out of the low word must be manually propagated into the high word — a real, if well-understood, implementation-complexity and constant-time-correctness burden); comparatively thin published cryptanalysis literature relative to 32/64-bit designs, flagged as a genuine open area in `09` |
