## 1. Research Methodology — How Successful ARX Papers Are Actually Conducted

### 1.1 How hypotheses are formed

Successful ARX research papers rarely begin with "let me invent a new cipher" in the abstract; they begin with a **specific, scoped technical question**: "does this specific rotation-constant search technique find better trails than the prior best-known method, on standard benchmark ciphers?" (the Window Heuristic paper, `05` §1.6); "does combining RX-cryptanalysis with the rectangle/boomerang framework yield attacks beyond what either technique achieves alone?" (`04` §2.4's rectangle paper); "can a neural network trained purely on ciphertext pairs match or exceed classical differential-distribution-table-based distinguishers?" (Gohr, `04` §2.10). This pattern — a scoped, falsifiable, comparison-against-prior-work question, not an open-ended "design a new secure thing" ambition — is the actual shape of nearly every paper this report's search record surfaced, and is a genuinely important, if unglamorous, lesson for a researcher whose stated ultimate goal (per your original prompt) is "design a new ARX permutation": **the field's actual publication currency is overwhelmingly incremental, comparison-anchored technical contributions**, not from-scratch new-cipher proposals evaluated only against the proposer's own informal judgment — a new-cipher paper that *does* get published successfully (Sparkle/Alzette being this report's central example, `02` §2.12) succeeds specifically because it's paired with a genuine, separately-evaluable methodological contribution (the long-trail strategy) that can be judged on its own terms independent of whether the specific cipher "wins" any subsequent competition.

### 1.2 Experimental design, controls, and statistical rigor

**Positive controls**: when reporting that a new tool/technique finds better trails/distinguishers than prior work, the expected practice is to *first* reproduce the prior best-known results using the new tool/technique (demonstrating the tool correctly replicates known-good results before trusting its novel findings) — a genuine, expected "does this tool work at all, verified against ground truth" step, directly analogous to a positive control in a wet-lab experiment. **Negative controls**: for a new cipher/permutation design specifically, an analogous check is to run the same security-evaluation toolchain against a *deliberately weakened* variant (fewer rounds, a known-bad rotation constant) and confirm the toolchain correctly flags it as weak — verifying the evaluation methodology isn't systematically blind to real weaknesses, not just confirming it agrees with intuition on the full-strength design. **Statistical rigor**: for any result involving empirical/statistical measurement (dependency-matrix SAC/BIC estimation per `03`, randomness-test-suite results per `06`, differential-neural distinguisher accuracy per `04` §2.10), current expected practice includes reporting sample sizes, confidence intervals or equivalent uncertainty quantification, and — for neural-distinguisher work specifically — train/validation/test-set separation discipline directly analogous to standard machine-learning research practice generally (a real, and sometimes under-observed, standard specifically worth flagging given how recently differential-neural cryptanalysis matured into the mainstream ARX literature).

### 1.3 Replication and cross-validation

**Replication**: reproducing a prior paper's specific numeric claims (a specific trail probability, a specific attack complexity) using independently-written code, not merely re-reading the paper and accepting its claims — a genuinely expected, if not always explicitly narrated, part of building on prior work in this literature, and the entire *reason* published reference implementations and, increasingly, published MILP/SAT model source code (per `05` §1.7 and 3.5 below) matter as much as they do: they make replication tractable for the next researcher rather than requiring an independent from-scratch reimplementation of the entire modeling pipeline. **Cross-validation across tools**: for load-bearing claims (a design's core security-margin bound), current best practice increasingly means verifying a key result via *more than one* independent tool/technique where feasible (an MILP-derived bound cross-checked against an independent SAT-based search, per `05` §2.4's discussion of hybrid methods) — not because either tool alone is untrustworthy, but because independent cross-validation catches modeling bugs (an incorrectly-encoded addition constraint, say) that a single tool's internal consistency checks cannot catch on their own.

### 1.4 Artifact evaluation

Increasingly formalized at top venues (CRYPTO, EUROCRYPT, and especially the more systems/implementation-oriented CHES) via explicit **artifact evaluation** processes — submitting not just the paper but the actual code/models/data underlying its claims for independent verification, sometimes resulting in a separate "artifacts evaluated" badge on the published paper. For ARX-specific submissions, this concretely means: the MILP/SAT model source code used to derive trail-search results, the reference and (where performance claims are made) optimized implementations, and, for differential-neural work, the training pipeline and (ideally) trained model weights or at least full training-reproduction instructions. A submission's persuasiveness to reviewers, per 2 below, is measurably higher when this material is available for independent checking rather than existing only as prose claims in the paper text.

## 2. Common Mistakes Made by Independent Researchers

Synthesizing across every technical file in this report, with historical examples woven in where directly available from this report's search record and from the broader field's well-documented cautionary-tale literature:

### 2.1 Poor metrics and weak benchmarks

**Confusing diffusion with security** (flagged explicitly in `03` §2.6, restated here as the report's single most important named mistake): reporting SAC/BIC/avalanche compliance as if it were equivalent to, rather than merely correlated with and insufficient evidence for, resistance to differential/linear cryptanalysis. This is, per this report's synthesis across the search record and general field discourse, the *most commonly repeated specific technical mistake* in informally-published/independent ARX design proposals — a design that "looks random" under avalanche testing but has never been subjected to genuine MILP/SAT trail search is not a security-evaluated design, regardless of how compelling its diffusion heatmaps look.

**Weak benchmarks and comparing against strawman baselines**: benchmarking a new design's *hand-tuned, heavily-optimized* implementation against a *competing design's unoptimized reference* implementation (flagged already in `07` §3.4) — a genuinely common, sometimes unintentional (the researcher may simply not have invested equal optimization effort in the comparison baseline) but nonetheless methodologically invalid comparison that experienced reviewers specifically watch for and will call out.

### 2.2 Confirmation bias and improper statistical testing

Running a battery of statistical/empirical tests and reporting only the subset that support the design's security claims, without disclosing the full test battery run — a general research-integrity issue, not ARX-specific, but with a specific ARX instantiation worth naming: running many candidate rotation-constant schedules against a cheap proxy metric (informal diffusion-speed inspection, say) and reporting only the best-found schedule's *full* MILP/SAT evaluation, without disclosing how many candidates were screened or what the screening criterion's own false-negative rate might be — a subtle, ARX-specific form of the general "researcher degrees of freedom" statistical-methodology concern.

### 2.3 Inadequate threat models

Designing and evaluating against differential/linear cryptanalysis alone, without explicit consideration of the fuller attack catalog `04` lays out (RX-cryptanalysis specifically, given its now-mature tooled status; differential-neural cryptanalysis, given its 2019–2025 maturation) — an inadequate-threat-model mistake that was arguably *reasonable* practice a decade ago (before RX-cryptanalysis and differential-neural techniques were mature/tooled) but is a genuine, current gap in any 2026-era design evaluation that stops at classical differential/linear analysis alone.

### 2.4 Ignoring prior art and overclaiming novelty

Proposing a "new" ARX round structure that is, on close inspection, structurally equivalent (under a simple relabeling, per `04` §1.3's rotation-constant-equivalence discussion) to an existing, already-analyzed design — a genuinely common failure mode given how constrained the ARX design space actually is at the operation level (only three possible operations, applied to a modest number of words), making genuine structural novelty harder to achieve, and harder to verify the absence of prior art for, than researchers new to the field sometimes appreciate. Overclaiming novelty specifically in the "provable security" dimension — claiming a wide-trail-strategy-style provable bound without actually doing the long-trail-strategy-equivalent work (`03` §1.4) to establish it, instead merely asserting the bound by informal analogy to AES/Sparkle's published results — is a specific, recurring version of this mistake worth calling out precisely, since "we use the wide-trail strategy" is sometimes asserted in informal ARX design write-ups without the actual ARX-box-probability-bound-establishment work (`03` §1.4's "two-part" requirement) having been done.

### 2.5 Confusing randomness with unpredictability, and misusing PractRand

Covered in full technical detail in `06` §2.2–2.4; restated here specifically as a *named, catalogued* research mistake: treating strong PractRand/TestU01 results as sufficient evidence of a PRNG design's cryptographic security, and — the more specific, PractRand-particular version of this mistake — running PractRand at an output-volume too small to have real statistical power against the specific flaw class of concern (`06` §2.4) and treating a "no anomaly" result at that under-powered sample size as meaningfully strong evidence.

### 2.6 Misinterpreting avalanche and poor experimental controls

A closely related but distinct mistake from 2.1: even when a researcher correctly avoids conflating avalanche/SAC with full security, a narrower version of the same error is common — computing avalanche/SAC statistics from too few random trials (an empirical-sampling-noise issue, `03` §2.8's final bullet) and treating small deviations from the ideal 0.5 dependency-matrix value as meaningful signal when they may simply be sampling noise at the tested sample size, or, conversely, dismissing a genuinely meaningful small-but-consistent deviation as "close enough" without the statistical-significance testing needed to actually distinguish the two cases.

### 2.7 Incomplete citations and weak reproducibility

Failing to cite the specific, current state-of-the-art prior work a new result should be compared against (citing, say, Gohr's original 2019 differential-neural paper without engaging with the substantial 2021–2025 follow-on literature covered in `04` §2.10, when that follow-on literature directly bears on the paper's central claims) — a real, specific, and easily-avoidable mistake given how actively and recently much of this literature has developed, and one reviewers at any of the venues in Part 3 below will notice immediately given how well-read they generally are in exactly this fast-moving space. **Weak reproducibility**: publishing numeric results (trail probabilities, attack complexities, benchmark cycles-per-byte figures) without the underlying code/models needed to independently verify them, per 1.4 above's artifact-evaluation discussion — increasingly treated as a real submission weakness, not merely a missed nicety, at the venues covered next.

## 3. Reviewer Mindset — CRYPTO, EUROCRYPT, FSE, ToSC, CHES, IACR ePrint

### 3.1 What these venues actually evaluate, and how they differ

- **CRYPTO / EUROCRYPT / ASIACRYPT** (the three flagship IACR general conferences): broad scope across all of cryptography, correspondingly high bar for *general* significance/novelty; an ARX-specific submission needs to clearly justify why its contribution matters beyond the narrow ARX-research community specifically (a genuinely new automated-search *technique*, or a design with broader methodological implications like Sparkle/Alzette's long-trail strategy, clears this bar more easily than an incremental new-cipher proposal alone would).
- **FSE (Fast Software Encryption) / ToSC (IACR Transactions on Symmetric Cryptology)**: FSE is now published as a ToSC special issue, and together these are **the** primary, most directly relevant venue for ARX-specific work — symmetric-cryptography-focused, with a reviewer pool deeply, specifically expert in exactly the differential/linear/RX/automated-search literature this report covers, and correspondingly the venue where the *overwhelming majority* of the specific papers cited throughout `04`–`05` were actually published. A new ARX permutation or a new ARX-cryptanalysis technique should, in nearly all cases, target ToSC/FSE as the natural first-choice venue.
- **CHES (Cryptographic Hardware and Embedded Systems)**: the flagship venue for implementation-security, side-channel, and hardware/lightweight-cryptography-deployment-focused work specifically — the natural venue for a paper primarily about an ARX design's *hardware* implementation, side-channel resistance, or lightweight-deployment characteristics (per `07`), as distinct from a paper primarily about the design's abstract cryptanalytic security margin (which belongs at ToSC/FSE instead).
- **IACR ePrint**: not a peer-reviewed venue at all, but the field's de facto rapid-preprint/permanent-archive server — essentially every paper eventually published at the venues above appears on ePrint first (often months before formal publication), and ePrint-only papers (never submitted to or not yet accepted at a formal venue) are common and legitimate, but carry correspondingly less validated-quality signal than a peer-reviewed publication — a distinction worth being precise about when citing ePrint-only material (as this report does throughout, with explicit peer-review-status flagging per the Executive Summary's confidence-rating discipline).

### 3.2 What evidence convinces reviewers

Directly following from `05` §3.5's "how modern papers justify security" synthesis: reviewers at FSE/ToSC/CHES specifically expect, as a baseline (not an aspirational extra): (a) explicit MILP/SAT-derived trail-search results with stated round-count margin beyond any found attack; (b) explicit treatment of the *current* attack catalog (`04`) — not just classical differential/linear, but RX-cryptanalysis and, increasingly, at least an acknowledgment of the differential-neural literature's relevance; (c) diffusion-metric results presented as *supporting*, not substituting for, (a); (d) for a wrapped construction (AEAD/hash/PRNG), a formal reduction/concrete-security argument (`05` §3); (e) reproducible artifacts (1.4 above); and (f) honest, explicit discussion of the design's *limitations* and *open questions* — reviewers in this specific research community are, per the general tenor of the papers surfacing throughout this report's search record, notably comfortable with (and generally reassured by, rather than alarmed by) papers that explicitly flag unresolved margin questions or acknowledge a specific attack family hasn't yet been fully evaluated, versus papers that implicitly or explicitly claim unqualified, complete security — the field's own epistemic norms (echoing this conversation's earlier general-cryptography report's "never hide uncertainty" instruction) reward calibrated, explicit uncertainty over false completeness.

### 3.3 What immediately raises red flags

- A new cipher/permutation proposal with **no MILP/SAT trail-search evidence at all**, relying solely on diffusion-metric (SAC/BIC/avalanche) testing — per 2.1 above, this is treated as a fundamentally incomplete security evaluation, not merely a weaker one, at any of the venues in 3.1.
- **Unsubstantiated "N-bit security" claims** asserted by analogy to state/key size alone, without a traceable concrete-security derivation (`05` §3.4).
- **Benchmark comparisons that don't disclose implementation-optimization-level parity** (`07` §3.4, 2.1 above) — reviewers experienced in this space actively look for exactly this kind of comparison-fairness issue.
- **Missing engagement with recent (2023–2025) literature** specifically in a fast-moving sub-area (differential-neural cryptanalysis, RX-cryptanalysis tooling, the Window Heuristic-style modeling advances) — given how actively and recently this exact literature has developed, a submission that reads as though it were written against a 2018-era understanding of the field's toolkit will be noticed immediately by reviewers who are, by the nature of who reviews for these specific venues, closely tracking exactly this literature.
- **Claiming a provable security bound without doing the actual bound-establishment work** (2.4 above's specific "asserting the wide/long-trail strategy by analogy" mistake).

### 3.4 What experiments are expected, and what cryptanalysis is mandatory

Synthesizing the entire report's technical content into a direct checklist, mirroring the "Deliverables" and "Experimental checklists" your original prompt specifically requested:

- [ ] Full specification with reference implementation (`02`, `07` §1.6)
- [ ] Diffusion-metric evaluation: SAC, BIC, avalanche, branch number (`03` §2)
- [ ] MILP or SAT-derived active-operation lower bounds and optimal-trail search across the full round count, with stated margin (`05` §1.5, §1.7)
- [ ] Explicit differential-linear/PNB-style combined-attack evaluation, not differential/linear in isolation (`04` §2.3)
- [ ] Explicit rotational-XOR (RX) cryptanalysis evaluation (`04` §2.4)
- [ ] Explicit consideration of boomerang/rectangle, impossible-differential, and meet-in-the-middle attack families, even if only to argue they don't apply or aren't the binding constraint (`04` §2.5–2.6)
- [ ] Explicit statement of the Markov-cipher-assumption's applicability and any sanity-check verification performed (`03` §1.5, `05` §1.7)
- [ ] Where relevant to the design's target use case, differential-neural distinguisher evaluation or at least explicit acknowledgment of this attack family's relevance (`04` §2.10)
- [ ] Formal reduction to the ideal-permutation/indifferentiability framework for any wrapped mode, with a concrete-security bound (`05` §3)
- [ ] Performance benchmarking with disclosed methodology (platform, compiler, optimization level, variance) across at least the portable-reference and, ideally, one SIMD-optimized implementation tier (`07` §3)
- [ ] For lightweight/embedded-targeted designs specifically, hardware footprint and energy figures (`07` §3.2)
- [ ] For PRNG designs specifically, explicit forward-secrecy, backward-secrecy, and fork-safety treatment, plus both internal-permutation cryptanalytic evaluation and separate output-stream statistical testing (`06` §1–2)
- [ ] Published, reproducible artifacts: models, code, and (for neural work) training pipelines (1.4 above)
- [ ] Explicit, honest discussion of open questions and unresolved margin (3.2 above)

### 3.5 What constitutes a publishable contribution

Restating 1.1's core finding as this file's closing synthesis: a publishable ARX contribution at FSE/ToSC/CHES-level rigor is, overwhelmingly in current practice, one of (a) a **genuinely new, independently-evaluable technique** (a new automated-search modeling improvement, a new attack-family combination, a new formal-security-methodology contribution like the long-trail strategy) that happens to be demonstrated against a new or existing design, rather than (b) a new design alone, evaluated only against the proposer's own analysis, with no independently-citable methodological contribution beyond "here is a cipher that survived my own testing." An independent researcher whose ultimate goal (per this report's Executive Summary) is to design and publish a genuinely novel ARX permutation should, per this file's synthesis, plan from the outset to pair the design with *at least one* genuinely independent, separately-defensible technical contribution — a new rotation-constant search method, a new diffusion-metric refinement, a novel wrapped-mode construction, or a rigorous new-methodology security argument — rather than treating "the design itself" as the sole intended contribution.
