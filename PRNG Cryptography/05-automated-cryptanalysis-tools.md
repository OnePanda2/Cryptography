## 1. MILP — From First Principles

### 1.1 How MILP became the standard tool

**Mixed-Integer Linear Programming** solves optimization problems over a mix of continuous and integer-valued variables subject to linear constraints, with a linear (or, for MIQCP extensions, quadratic) objective. Its adoption as *the* standard cryptanalysis tool traces to **Mouha, Wang, Gu, Preneel (2011)**, who first modeled the problem of counting the minimum number of active S-boxes in a differential trail (the wide-trail-strategy core computation, `03` §1.4) as an MILP instance, and **Sun, Hu, Wang, Wang, Qiao, Ma, Song (2014)**, who extended this to full bit-level differential/linear characteristic search including, critically for this report, **ARX's modular addition operator specifically**. The reason MILP won out over hand-derived search almost immediately: once a cipher's round function is correctly encoded as a set of linear inequalities over binary/integer variables, a mature, industrial-grade commercial solver (Gurobi, CPLEX) can search the *entire* trail space for the provably-optimal (minimum-weight, i.e., maximum-probability) trail, or prove a tight lower bound on the number of active nonlinear operations, far beyond what any hand-guided search could hope to verify exhaustively — turning what was previously a craft-skill, error-prone manual process into a mechanically-verifiable, reproducible computation.

### 1.2 Modeling XOR

XOR of two n-bit words is modeled bit-by-bit: for binary variables a, b representing input difference bits and c representing the output difference bit, the constraint c = a ⊕ b is expressed as a small, standard set of linear inequalities (e.g., a + b + c ≤ 2, a + b − c ≥ 0, and the symmetric variants) — a completely standard, non-controversial piece of MILP modeling with no ARX-specific subtlety, since XOR is linear (`01`, §2.4).

### 1.3 Modeling rotation

Rotation by a fixed, known amount is modeled trivially, since it's just a fixed relabeling of which difference-bit variable corresponds to which output position — no new constraints are needed beyond correctly indexing the existing bit-variables under the rotation; rotation contributes literally zero complexity to an MILP model, which is worth noting explicitly since it underscores that **all of the real modeling difficulty in ARX MILP work is concentrated entirely in modeling modular addition** (1.4 below).

### 1.4 Modeling modular addition — the actual hard problem

This is the single technically central piece of ARX automated cryptanalysis, and every serious ARX researcher should understand it at least at the level presented here. Given the Lipmaa–Moriai exact differential-probability formula for addition (`01`, §2.5), the goal is to encode, as a set of linear inequalities over the input-difference bits α, β, output-difference bits γ, and (for computing an actual probability weight, not just feasibility) an auxiliary "weight" variable, exactly which (α,β,γ) triples are differentially possible for addition and what their probability (expressed as −log2 of the probability, so that *multiplying* probabilities across rounds becomes *summing* weights — the standard MILP-objective-linearization trick used throughout this entire literature) is. Because addition's differential behavior depends on a bit-level carry automaton (three possible per-bit states: carry-generating, carry-propagating, carry-absorbing, per `01` §2.5), a **direct, correct** MILP encoding requires either (a) a moderately large but still linear-sized set of inequalities per bit position, correctly capturing the automaton's transition structure (the approach Sun et al. 2014 and most follow-on work take), or (b) an *approximation* — most commonly, **linearizing** the addition as XOR (treating it as if it were linear) for the purposes of a first-pass, cheaper search, then verifying/refining the found candidate trails against the true, non-linearized addition model — the "partial linearization trade-off" explicitly named in the 2025 "Window Heuristic" paper (1.6 below) as its central technical contribution: efficiently trading off how much of the addition's true nonlinear behavior to model exactly (expensive, more accurate) versus approximately-linearize (cheap, requires more downstream verification) at different points in the search.

### 1.5 Finding differential and linear trails, lower and upper bounds

Once the round function is fully encoded, the MILP objective is typically **minimize total weight** (equivalently, maximize total probability) summed across all active operations over the target number of rounds, subject to the constraint that the trail is nonzero (some initial difference is injected) — solving this gives the **provably-optimal** (best-possible) trail for that many rounds, which directly gives an **upper bound on the actual best-attack probability achievable via this trail-search technique** (an attacker cannot do better than the optimal trail the model found, *assuming the Markov-cipher-assumption caveat from `03` §1.5 holds*). Separately, MILP is used to derive **lower bounds on the number of active operations** (rather than searching for the single best trail, prove that *every* trail must have at least k active operations, giving a security-margin argument even without fully enumerating trails) — this is the direct ARX/general-automated-search analogue of the wide/long-trail strategy's provable-bound methodology (`03` §1.4), and is generally the more *design-time-relevant* computation (a designer wants "prove no trail below weight W exists for R rounds," not merely "here is one particular trail we found").

### 1.6 Best software, open-source frameworks, and reference papers

- **Gurobi** and **CPLEX**: the dominant commercial MILP solvers used throughout this literature (academic licenses are freely available and near-universal in the field); genuinely industrial-strength, capable of solving MILP instances with many thousands of variables/constraints within practical time for real cipher-scale trail search.
- **CryptoSMT** (Stefan Kölbl): an open-source, purpose-built framework specifically for cryptanalytic SAT/SMT modeling (bridging into the SAT/SMT section, 2 below, but frequently used alongside/interchangeably with MILP-based tools in practice) — referenced directly in this report's search record as the underlying tool for several published QRE-ChaCha and Speck-family differential searches.
- **CLAASP** (Cryptographic Library for the Automated Analysis of Symmetric Primitives; Bellini et al., ePrint 2023/622): a unified, actively-maintained open-source library specifically designed to let a researcher define a *new* ARX (or other symmetric) primitive once and automatically generate MILP/SAT models for differential, linear, and several other analysis types against it — directly aimed at exactly this report's target audience (a researcher designing a *new* permutation who needs to evaluate it, not just re-run analysis on an existing well-studied one), and the single most directly actionable piece of current tooling this report can point to.
- **The Window Heuristic** (Bellini, Gerault, Grados, Peyrin, CT-RSA 2025, building on ePrint 2024/1743): the most recent (as of this report's writing) advance specifically in *how* to model modular addition efficiently, via the partial-linearization trade-off described in 1.4 above — applied to ChaCha, SPECK, LEA, and HIGHT, finding the first 4-round ChaCha differential path with probability above 2^-256 and a corresponding 6-round boomerang distinguisher, described by the authors as outperforming all previous modular-addition modeling approaches.
- **Reference/foundational papers**: Mouha, Wang, Gu, Preneel (2011, active-S-box MILP counting); Sun, Hu, Wang, Wang, Qiao, Ma, Song (2014, full ARX bit-level MILP modeling, "Automatic Security Evaluation and (Related-key) Differential Characteristic Search"); Fu, Wang, Guo, Sun, Hu (FSE 2016, MILP-based automatic search for Speck specifically); Liu, Fu, Guo, et al. (independent-inputs/independent-rounds assumption refinements, several 2018–2019 papers per this report's search record, including a specific Bel-T-cipher-focused carry-bit-level refinement).

### 1.7 Common mistakes and a research roadmap

**Common mistakes**: (1) using the naive "independent inputs, independent rounds" simplifying assumption (standard in early MILP-modeling papers) without checking whether it materially affects the result for a specific design — later work (per 1.6's reference list) has shown this assumption can meaningfully mis-estimate true probability for some ciphers, and a rigorous current evaluation should at least acknowledge and, ideally, verify against this; (2) reporting a found trail's MILP-computed probability as the *attack's* actual complexity without separately accounting for the Markov-assumption gap (`03` §1.5) and for the cost of the surrounding key-recovery procedure (a trail's raw distinguishing probability is not the same number as a full key-recovery attack's time complexity); (3) treating "MILP found no trail below weight W" as equivalent to "no attack exists," when it may only mean "no trail *of the specific form the model searches for* exists" — a model that only searches single-key differential trails, say, says nothing about differential-linear, RX-differential, or differential-neural attacks, all of which require *separate* automated-search evaluation (per `04`). **Research roadmap**: for a new design, the expected minimum current-practice MILP workflow is: build the addition/XOR/rotation model in a framework like CLAASP; compute active-operation lower bounds across the target round count; find the actual optimal trail (not just the bound) for at least several rounds beyond where the bound suggests security is reached, as a margin-verification step; and explicitly extend the model (or use a companion tool) to check RX-differential and, where relevant, differential-linear structure specifically, not stopping at pure single-key XOR-differential trail search alone.

## 2. SAT, SMT, and Constraint Programming

### 2.1 SAT and SMT, explained for this context

**SAT** (Boolean satisfiability) asks whether a given Boolean formula, typically in conjunctive normal form (CNF), has a satisfying assignment; **SMT** (Satisfiability Modulo Theories) generalizes this to formulas involving richer theories (bit-vectors, arithmetic) beyond pure Boolean logic, letting modular addition be expressed *natively* as a bit-vector addition constraint rather than requiring the operation to be manually decomposed into CNF clauses the way pure-SAT encoding requires — a genuine modeling-convenience advantage SMT has over both pure SAT and MILP for expressing ARX operations directly, at the cost of SMT solvers generally being somewhat less mature/optimized for this specific cryptanalytic search-and-optimize workload than either dedicated MILP solvers (Gurobi) or purpose-tuned SAT solvers.

### 2.2 CP-SAT and constraint programming

**Constraint Programming (CP)**, and Google OR-Tools' **CP-SAT** solver specifically, offers a third modeling paradigm — expressing the problem via arbitrary constraint relations (not restricted to strictly linear inequalities the way MILP is, nor to strictly Boolean clauses the way pure SAT is) combined with a highly-optimized hybrid SAT/CP search engine — increasingly used in the current cryptanalysis-tooling literature as a genuinely competitive alternative to both MILP and pure SAT, particularly for problems (like some of the RX-differential search framework work referenced in `04` §2.4) that are naturally expressed via CNF-style clauses but benefit from CP-SAT's more flexible constraint language and strong built-in optimization search.

### 2.3 Automatic cryptanalysis pipelines and modern SAT tooling

Modern cryptanalytic SAT usage almost always means a **purpose-built pipeline** wrapping a general SAT solver (CryptoMiniSat being the most commonly cited in the general-cryptography literature — general-cryptography-report Part `04` §1.4 — and equally standard here), not raw, unassisted general-purpose SAT solving: **CryptoSMT** (2.4/1.6 above) provides exactly this kind of purpose-built cryptanalysis-specific SAT/SMT modeling layer, letting a researcher describe a cipher's round function at a reasonably high level and have the tool generate and dispatch the correct low-level clauses.

### 2.4 Comparison with MILP, and hybrid methods

| Dimension | MILP | SAT/SMT | CP-SAT |
|---|---|---|---|
| Native handling of addition | Requires explicit bit-level inequality encoding (1.4 above) | SMT can express bit-vector addition natively; pure SAT requires CNF decomposition like MILP | Native constraint expression, flexible |
| Solver maturity for this workload | Very high (Gurobi/CPLEX are industrial-grade, widely benchmarked in this exact literature) | High but more variable across specific solvers/problem instances | Increasingly competitive, actively improving |
| Typical use case in current literature | Optimal-trail search, active-operation lower bounds | Trail search, especially where CNF/clause-based modeling is natural (e.g., RX-differential search per `04` §2.4) | Emerging as a strong alternative, especially for RX-style and other CNF-natural problems |
| Provable optimality | Yes, when the solver terminates | Yes, when the solver terminates (UNSAT proof for infeasibility) | Yes |

**Hybrid methods**: a real, increasingly common current practice (reflected directly in the Window Heuristic's partial-linearization approach and in several of the 2023–2025 RX-search papers referenced in `04`) is to use a *cheaper*, approximate or partially-linearized model (often SAT/CP-based, for speed) to prune the search space down to a manageable set of promising candidate trail structures, then verify/refine the surviving candidates against a *fully accurate* (typically MILP-based, or an exact bit-level simulation) model — trading off search speed against modeling exactness in a principled, staged way rather than committing to one tool/one exactness level for the entire search.

### 2.5 Open-source tools, benchmark suites, and research papers

Beyond CryptoSMT and CLAASP (already covered): the **broader eSTREAM/CAESAR/NIST-LWC competition archives** themselves function as de facto benchmark suites, since essentially every automated-search tooling paper in this literature validates its tool against the same handful of well-known targets (Speck, Simon, ChaCha, LEA, HIGHT, SIMECK) specifically because doing so lets a new paper's results be directly, numerically compared against the substantial existing body of prior published results for those same ciphers — a genuinely useful, if somewhat homogenizing, community norm worth following when evaluating a *new* tool or technique (benchmark against these same standard targets first, before applying the tool to your own novel design, so reviewers have a familiar point of comparison).

## 3. Formal Security Evaluation

### 3.1 Game-based and provable security in the ARX/permutation context

Where an ARX construction is used to build a *keyed* primitive with a well-defined cryptographic goal (a MAC, an AEAD scheme, a PRF like SipHash), the same game-based security framework covered in Part 1 of this conversation's general-cryptography report applies directly — IND-CPA/CCA-style indistinguishability games, EUF-CMA for MAC/signature-adjacent constructions — with the ARX-specific work being to actually *carry out* the reduction proof against the specific round function's properties (typically requiring the round function to be modeled as an ideal primitive for the proof to go through, per 3.2 below, since a "real" ARX round function's exact behavior is generally too complex to reason about directly inside a reduction proof).

### 3.2 The ideal permutation model

Analogous to the random oracle model (general-cryptography report, Part `01` §2.6) but for permutations rather than functions: model the underlying ARX permutation (Xoodoo, the Sparkle permutation, ChaCha's core permutation before its stream-cipher wrapping) as a **truly random permutation** that all parties, including the adversary, can query (and, since it's a permutation, invert) — letting security proofs for constructions built *on top of* the permutation (a sponge-based hash or AEAD scheme, say) proceed without needing to reason about the permutation's actual internal ARX structure directly. This is the standard, expected proof framework for any new sponge/duplex-based ARX construction's formal security argument, exactly paralleling how Keccak/SHA-3's security proofs work (general-cryptography report, Part `02` §4.2).

### 3.3 Indifferentiability

The specific formal tool (Maurer, Renner, Holenstein, 2004; adapted to the sponge setting by Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters, Van Assche) used to rigorously justify treating a sponge construction built from an ideal permutation as itself behaving like a random oracle for the purposes of any *outer* security proof relying on that assumption — the standard justification underlying "sponge-based hash function X is secure in the ROM, given that its underlying permutation is ideal," and the specific piece of formal machinery a new sponge-based ARX permutation's security writeup is expected to invoke (or explicitly, carefully distinguish from, if the construction departs from a standard sponge/duplex mode in some way).

### 3.4 Distinguishing advantage and concrete security

Beyond asymptotic ("negligible advantage") security statements, the field's actual current practice — and specifically what a CRYPTO/FSE/ToSC reviewer expects, per `08` — is **concrete security**: an explicit numeric bound on an adversary's distinguishing/forgery advantage as a function of the number of queries, the number of rounds, and the found trail probabilities from the MILP/SAT analysis (1–2 above) — directly connecting the automated-cryptanalysis-tooling results to the formal security statement, rather than leaving them as two separate, unconnected pieces of the paper. A design's "N-bit security" claim should be traceable, in a rigorous submission, to a specific concrete-security bound derived this way, not asserted by analogy to the state/key size alone.

### 3.5 How modern papers justify security, and reviewer expectations

Synthesizing 1–4 above into what a current, publishable security-justification section for a new ARX permutation actually contains, and previewing `08`'s fuller reviewer-mindset treatment: (a) an explicit MILP/SAT-derived active-operation lower bound and/or optimal-trail-probability table across the design's round count, with clearly stated margin beyond the point where the bound suggests security is reached; (b) explicit treatment of the differential-linear, RX-differential, and (increasingly, per `04` §2.10) differential-neural attack families specifically, not merely a single differential/linear trail-search table; (c) a diffusion-metric evaluation (`03`) presented as *supporting*, not *substituting for*, the trail-search evidence; (d) where the construction wraps the permutation into a mode (AEAD, hash, PRNG), a formal reduction to the ideal-permutation/indifferentiability framework (3.2–3.3) giving a concrete-security bound (3.4); and (e), increasingly expected as standard practice rather than optional polish, reference implementations and machine-checkable models (the CLAASP-style "define once, analyze automatically" workflow) that let independent reviewers and future researchers *reproduce* the security claims directly, rather than merely reading a paper's prose description of them — directly anticipating `08`'s discussion of reproducibility as a core current reviewer expectation.
