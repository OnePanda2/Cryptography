## 1. Rotation Constants

### 1.1 The search problem

Choosing a design's rotation constants (and, jointly, its rotation *schedule* — which constant applies at which point in the round structure) is a discrete optimization problem: the search space for a single n-bit word's rotation amount is n−1 non-trivial choices (0 and n both being no-ops), but the *joint* search space across a full rotation schedule (multiple rotation operations per round, potentially varying round to round) grows combinatorially, quickly exceeding what exhaustive search can cover once a design has more than a handful of rotation operations — exactly the situation ChaCha's four-rotation-constant quarter-round schedule (16, 12, 8, 7) or Speck's per-variant rotation-pair choices represent.

### 1.2 Search methods

- **Exhaustive search**: fully tractable only for designs with very few independent rotation constants and small word sizes — evaluating every candidate schedule against a fixed diffusion/security metric (SAC convergence speed, best-found differential trail probability via a fast approximate search) and selecting the best. This was, in essence, how early ARX designs' rotation constants were chosen (informally, by hand-guided exhaustive-adjacent search over a small candidate set), and remains viable today specifically for narrow sub-problems (e.g., searching Alzette-scale ARX-box rotation pairs) rather than full-design-scale search.
- **MILP-assisted search**: rather than exhaustively evaluating every candidate schedule against an expensive full trail search, formulate rotation-constant selection *itself* as (or as a sub-objective within) an MILP model — searching for the schedule that *minimizes* the best-found differential/linear trail probability the MILP solver can locate, effectively using the `05`-covered automated-trail-search machinery as the fitness function inside an outer optimization loop over rotation-constant choices.
- **SAT-based search**: analogous to MILP-assisted search, but using a SAT/SMT solver's trail-finding capability as the inner fitness evaluator instead of an MILP solver — the choice between MILP- and SAT-based outer-loop search generally follows the same MILP-vs-SAT tradeoffs covered in full in `05`.
- **Evolutionary/heuristic search**: genetic algorithms, simulated annealing, and related metaheuristics applied directly to the rotation-constant search space, using a cheaper proxy fitness function (SAC/BIC convergence speed, or a fast approximate/bounded trail search rather than a full optimal-trail solve) to make the search computationally tractable at the cost of not guaranteeing the found schedule is provably optimal — a common, pragmatic middle ground between full exhaustive search (intractable at scale) and full MILP/SAT-optimal search (expensive per candidate evaluation) for designs with many independent rotation parameters.

### 1.3 Search spaces, equivalent constants, and symmetries

A naive rotation-constant search space is often substantially larger than the *effective* search space, because many candidate schedules are cryptographically equivalent to each other under simple transformations: **word-reversal symmetry** (rotating left by k is equivalent, under a global bit-reversal relabeling, to rotating right by k, and designs are often symmetric under such relabelings in ways that make two nominally-different rotation constants produce identical security properties); **rotation-amount complementation** (rotating left by k on an n-bit word is the same operation as rotating right by n−k, meaning a search need only consider roughly half the naive range); and, most importantly for the RX-cryptanalysis-aware search described in 1.4 below, **rotation constants that are simple fractions or multiples of the word size** (e.g., rotating a 32-bit word by exactly 16, or by any amount sharing a large common factor with 32) tend to preserve more structural symmetry and are generally *avoided* in good designs specifically because that preserved symmetry is exactly what rotational and RX-cryptanalysis (1.5 below and the attack-catalog Part 2) exploit — pruning the search space to exclude such "obviously symmetric" candidates before running expensive MILP/SAT-based evaluation on the remainder is standard, time-saving practice.

### 1.4 Why good constants work, why bad ones fail

A good rotation schedule achieves two things simultaneously, and a design's rotation-constant choice should be justified against both explicitly, not just one:

1. **Fast diffusion** — few rounds needed to reach full SAC/BIC-compliant dependency (Part `03`), achieved by choosing rotation amounts that, in combination with the design's word-interconnection structure, avoid leaving any output-bit subset "isolated" from some input-bit subset for many rounds. Poor choices (e.g., all rotation amounts sharing a common factor with the word size) can leave structured, slow-diffusing sub-lattices of bit positions that only fully mix after many more rounds than a well-chosen schedule would require.
2. **Resistance to rotational/RX-cryptanalysis specifically** — rotation amounts (and their interaction with round-constant placement, covered in 1.5) should not preserve any nontrivial rotational symmetry of the state that an adversary could track through many rounds at unusually high probability. A rotation schedule where every operation uses the *same* amount, for instance, is considerably more exploitable via rotational-style analysis than a schedule using several different, carefully-chosen amounts (part of why ChaCha's schedule uses four distinct values — 16, 12, 8, 7 — rather than one repeated value, and part of why this specific choice is worth understanding as deliberate, not arbitrary).

**Bad constants fail** in one of two observable ways: either the design needs unexpectedly many extra rounds to reach adequate diffusion (a performance failure, caught early by SAC/BIC testing), or — more dangerously, since it's not caught by diffusion testing alone per `03`'s blind-spot discussion — the design retains an exploitable rotational/RX-symmetric structure that only surfaces under dedicated rotational/RX-cryptanalysis (1.5 below), potentially long after initial publication, exactly the pattern several of the RX-cryptanalysis papers in this report's search record describe finding in previously-published designs' rotation schedules.

### 1.5 Current best practices and open research

Current (2023–2025) best practice, synthesized from this report's search record: rotation-constant selection should be validated jointly against (a) a full diffusion-metric evaluation (`03`), (b) a full MILP/SAT differential/linear trail search at the design's target round count with real margin, *and* (c) an explicit rotational-XOR-cryptanalysis check specifically (given RX-cryptanalysis's now-mature, tooled status per 2.4 below) — treating RX-resistance as a check separate from, not subsumed by, ordinary differential/linear trail search, since RX-cryptanalysis exploits a genuinely different symmetry than plain XOR-differential propagation does. **Open research**, flagged again in `09`: rotation-constant search methodology specifically for word sizes above 64 bits remains comparatively underdeveloped in the current literature (per this report's Executive Summary gap-flag), and joint optimization of rotation constants *and* round-constant placement *and* round count as a single combined search problem (rather than the more common practice of fixing round-constant placement first and searching rotation constants against it) is a real, tractable near-term research target for exactly the kind of independent researcher this report is written for.

## 2. Cryptanalysis of ARX — The Complete Attack Catalog

For every attack family: the idea, requirements, strengths/weaknesses, automation status, and current (2023–2025) research state specifically against ARX targets.

### 2.1 Differential cryptanalysis (ARX-specific)

**Idea**: track a fixed input XOR-difference through the cipher, exploiting the fact that modular addition's differential probability DP⁺(α,β→γ), per the Lipmaa–Moriai formula (`01`, §2.5), is non-uniform across output values — some (α,β,γ) triples have measurably higher-than-2^(-n) probability. **Requirements**: chosen plaintexts/inputs (or, for a permutation, chosen state inputs). **Strengths**: the field's best-understood, most-tooled ARX attack; exact probability computation via Lipmaa–Moriai makes automated search (`05`) both principled and tractable. **Weaknesses**: for well-designed ARX ciphers with adequate round count, no differential trail with usable probability exists across the full cipher — the attack's power is entirely a function of how many rounds it can be pushed through, which is exactly what automated search is used to bound. **Automation**: fully automated via MILP/SAT (`05`); manual differential-trail search for anything beyond toy scale is no longer considered adequate evidence at a serious venue. **Current research**: differential trails remain the base layer nearly every more sophisticated attack (differential-linear, boomerang, RX-differential) builds on; the 2024–2025 "Window Heuristic" (`05`) specifically improves *pure* differential-trail search quality for ARX designs including ChaCha, finding, per this report's search record, the first 4-round ChaCha differential path with probability above 2^-256.

### 2.2 Linear cryptanalysis (ARX-specific)

**Idea**: the direct analogue of differential cryptanalysis for linear approximations rather than XOR differences — find a linear combination of input/output/key bits holding with bias significantly different from 0, exploiting modular addition's non-uniform linear-correlation structure (analogous to, but distinct from, its differential-probability structure). **Requirements**: known plaintexts (weaker requirement than differential's chosen-plaintext need, per the general discussion in Part `04` of this conversation's earlier general-cryptography report). **Automation**: fully automated via the same MILP/SAT toolchain, adapted to linear-correlation modeling rather than differential-probability modeling. **Current research**: pure linear cryptanalysis against ARX targets is somewhat less prominent in isolation than differential-linear *combined* attacks (2.3 below), which have proven considerably more powerful against ChaCha/Salsa20 specifically than either pure technique alone.

### 2.3 Differential-linear cryptanalysis — the dominant ChaCha/Salsa20 attack line

**Idea**: chain a high-probability *differential* characteristic through the first several rounds with a high-*correlation linear* approximation through the remaining rounds, connected via the differential-linear framework (Langford & Hellman, 1994; refined substantially for the ARX setting by Beierle, Leander, Todo's CRYPTO 2020 work), combined with **Probabilistic Neutral Bits (PNBs)** — a technique, originating with Aumasson, Fischer, Khazaei, Meier, Rechberger at FSE 2008, that identifies key bits whose exact value has little effect on the attack's success probability, letting the attacker guess (or ignore) them cheaply while focusing exhaustive search on the smaller set of bits that matter. **This is, concretely, the specific technique responsible for essentially every headline ChaCha/Salsa20 cryptanalysis result of the past decade-plus**, and its steady 2023–2025 refinement is this report's central "what's currently moving" cryptanalysis narrative:
- **Beierle, Leander, Todo, CRYPTO 2020**: a 3.5-round differential (exploiting carefully-chosen key-IV pairs) that substantially improved the differential half of the attack chain.
- **Coutinho & Peyrin, ToSC 2023**: "Boosting differential-linear cryptanalysis of ChaCha7 with MILP" — the first work to apply MILP-based automated search specifically to *optimizing the differential-linear distinguisher itself* (not just the pure-differential component), rather than relying on hand-derived distinguishers.
- **Bellini et al., ToSC 2023**: introduced a 5-round differential-linear distinguisher incorporating a 2-bit initial difference (rather than the single-bit differences essentially all prior work had used) — a genuine methodological broadening of the attack's search space.
- **Dey, Garai, Maitra et al. (multiple 2022–2023 papers, including "Latin Dances Reloaded," ASIACRYPT 2022)**: refined PNB-selection algorithms specifically, improving attack complexity on both Salsa20 and ChaCha by factors described in this report's search record as roughly 2–5× over prior best results, and also proposed a new cipher, **Forró**, as a design-side response.
- **Xu, Xu, Tan, Qi, ToSC 2024**: the current published state of the art at the time of this report's search — improving the four-round differential-linear distinguisher's correlation via additional intermediate linear masks, then combining with a refined PNB assignment to reach **7-round and 7.25-round ChaCha** key-recovery attacks (complexity 2^189.7 and 2^223.9 respectively), and, via an equivalence argument between (R+0.25)-round and (R+0.5)-round variants, extending to **7.5-round ChaCha**.
- **Flórez-Gutiérrez & Todo, EUROCRYPT 2025**: "Improved Cryptanalysis of ChaCha: Beating PNBs with Bit Puncturing" — a further methodological refinement (per this report's search record) improving on the PNB framework itself via a new "bit puncturing" technique, representing the most recent published advance in this line as of this report's writing.

**Requirements**: chosen plaintext/IV, substantial (though not remotely practically-feasible against full ChaCha20) computational resources. **What this does and doesn't mean, stated plainly**: this is a real, active, technically-sophisticated, steadily-advancing research program — and it remains **12.5 of 20 rounds short** of the deployed cipher, meaning ChaCha20-Poly1305 as used in TLS 1.3/WireGuard/Signal is not remotely threatened by any of this work. It is, however, *exactly* the technique and complexity trajectory any new ChaCha-family or ChaCha-inspired design proposal must be benchmarked against and must budget explicit round-count margin beyond.

### 2.4 Rotational and rotational-XOR (RX) cryptanalysis

**Idea (rotational)**: track a *rotational pair* of inputs — (x, x≪r) for some fixed rotation amount r — through the cipher, exploiting that rotation commutes exactly with XOR and *approximately*, with a computable bias, with modular addition (since addition's carry chain is *not* exactly rotation-invariant, though it's close for favorable bit-position combinations). **Idea (RX, rotational-XOR)**: Ashur & Liu's FSE 2016 generalization, tracking a pair related by *both* rotation and an XOR difference simultaneously — (x, (x≪r)⊕α) — specifically to correctly handle round-constant injection, which breaks pure rotational symmetry (a rotation-invariant round constant would defeat the entire point of injecting it to break symmetry, per `01`, §2.6) but can still be tracked correctly under the more general RX-difference framework. **Current research (2023–2025), specifically flagged in this report's Executive Summary as a fast-moving thread**: RX-cryptanalysis has matured substantially from a largely hand-derived, small-scale technique into a fully-tooled, automated-search-driven attack class:
- **Automated RX-differential search frameworks** (per this report's search record, a 2025 Designs, Codes and Cryptography paper presenting a CNF/SAT-clause-based automatic RX-differential search framework) have found **17-round and 24-round RX-differential characteristics against Speck64/128, Speck96/144, and Speck128/256**, substantially extending the previously-longest hand-derived characteristics, which covered only 13 rounds for each — a genuinely large, tooling-driven jump.
- **Rotational-XOR differential rectangle cryptanalysis** (Chen, Zhu, Xiang, Xu, Zeng, Zhang, CT-RSA 2023): combines RX-cryptanalysis with the boomerang/rectangle framework (2.6 below), reaching 16–21-round distinguishers against Simon/Simeck variants — the first work to combine these two attack families specifically.
- **Rotational differential-linear cryptanalysis** (per this report's search record): extends the differential-linear framework of 2.3 above by replacing the differential component specifically with an RX-differential, a natural, actively-pursued generalization directly connecting this subsection to 2.3's dominant ChaCha attack line.
- **Deep-learning-enhanced RX attacks** (per this report's search record, a 2025 arXiv paper specifically on Simon32/64 and Simeck32/64): combines RX-cryptanalysis with the differential-neural methodology of 2.10 below — a genuinely current (as of this report's writing) convergence of two previously-separate 2023–2025-active research threads.
**Weaknesses/requirements**: RX-cryptanalysis's power depends heavily on round-constant placement specifically defeating or failing to defeat the exploited rotational symmetry — a design with well-chosen, genuinely non-rotation-invariant round constants (verified via the automated RX-search tooling above, not merely by informal inspection) substantially limits this attack family's reach, which is precisely why 1.5 above recommends explicit RX-cryptanalysis checking as a mandatory, separate design-validation step.

### 2.5 Boomerang and impossible differential attacks (ARX context)

**Boomerang attacks** (Part `04` of the general cryptography report covers the base technique) apply to ARX designs by chaining two shorter, higher-probability differential characteristics through each half of the cipher — often more effective against ARX ciphers specifically *because* ARX's carry-chain-limited nonlinearity per round means a single differential trail's probability tends to decay faster over many rounds than a boomerang's two-shorter-trails structure does, making boomerang-style attacks disproportionately competitive against ARX relative to pure single-trail differential attacks in some parameter regimes. **Impossible differential attacks** (find a zero-probability differential over some rounds, use it as a key-elimination sieve) apply identically in structure to the general technique, with the zero-probability trail itself found via the same automated-search tooling (MILP/SAT models can search specifically for *provably impossible* propagations, not just high-probability ones, by adding constraints forcing probability to exactly zero and checking feasibility).

### 2.6 Meet-in-the-middle attacks (ARX context)

Applicable to ARX designs with a decomposable structure (splitting the cipher into forward-computable and backward-computable halves) exactly as in the general SPN/Feistel case (general-cryptography report, Part `04`); less centrally prominent in the current ARX literature than differential-linear and RX-cryptanalysis specifically, but a standard part of the attack toolkit any thorough security evaluation should explicitly rule out for a candidate new design, especially one with an easily-decomposable round structure.

### 2.7 Related-key attacks

Especially relevant for ARX ciphers whose key schedule is itself simple/linear (a common ARX design choice, since a complex key schedule adds implementation cost without necessarily adding much security if the round function itself is strong) — a weak key schedule can let an attacker exploit chosen or known relationships between keys even when the round function alone would resist single-key attacks, exactly the general lesson from the WEP/related-key discussion in this conversation's earlier general-cryptography report (Part `04`), applied specifically to the ARX key-schedule-design decision.

### 2.8 Invariant attacks

Find a nonlinear (or affine) subspace/property of the ARX state preserved by the round function regardless of key value — a real, historically-damaging attack class against some lightweight ciphers generally (general-cryptography report, Part `04`, §1.3), and a specific concern for ARX designs using unusually simple or structured round-constant sequences, since invariant attacks often exploit exactly the kind of residual structure a poorly-randomized constant sequence leaves behind.

### 2.9 Integral, cube, and algebraic (SAT/MILP-as-attack) methods

**Integral attacks** apply to ARX designs analogously to the general technique (general-cryptography report, Part `04`, §1.3), tracking structured sets of inputs through the carry-chain-limited addition operations; **cube attacks** (Dinur–Shamir) are particularly relevant for ARX stream ciphers and lightweight designs with low per-round algebraic degree, treating output bits as low-degree polynomials in key/IV bits — a standard part of the lightweight-cryptography evaluation toolkit any Ascon/Sparkle/TinyJambu-class design candidate is checked against. **SAT-based and MILP-based algebraic attacks**, distinct from their *trail-search* use in `05`, can also be applied directly as attacks in their own right — encoding the full cipher (not just a differential/linear trail model) as a satisfiability or optimization instance and attempting to solve for the key directly, generally only tractable against significantly reduced-round variants but a standard, expected part of a thorough evaluation.

### 2.10 Statistical distinguishers and differential-neural cryptanalysis

**Statistical distinguishers** generally: any technique that distinguishes cipher output from random via a statistical test not captured by the classical differential/linear framework specifically — a broad catch-all category, but increasingly dominated in the current ARX literature by:

**Differential-neural cryptanalysis**, the field's most significant recent (2019–2025) methodological addition to the attack catalog:
- **Gohr, CRYPTO 2019**: the founding result — train a neural network directly as a differential distinguisher against reduced-round Speck32/64, achieving distinguishing accuracy exceeding what the classical differential distribution table alone would predict, then using the trained distinguisher inside a key-recovery attack pipeline, achieving results competitive with or exceeding the best classical attacks at the time.
- **Gohr's own "Real Differences Experiment" and "aaaa-blinding" experiment**: methodologically crucial follow-up work specifically investigating *what the neural network is actually learning* — showing neural distinguishers can detect real (not just difference-based) structure in ciphertext-pair distributions beyond what the classical differential distribution table captures, but that this extra power disappears under certain blinding transformations, an important, still-actively-discussed (2023 "More Insight on Deep Learning-Aided Cryptanalysis," AICrypt/ASIACRYPT workshop) open question about exactly how much of differential-neural distinguishers' power is genuinely novel versus a repackaging of known differential/differential-linear structure.
- **Benamira et al., EUROCRYPT 2021**: "A Deeper Look at Machine Learning-Based Cryptanalysis" — further dissecting the neural distinguisher's learned behavior.
- **DbitNet** (Bellini et al., per this report's search record, ~2023): a new, **architecture-agnostic** neural-distinguisher design, providing new differential-neural distinguishers across multiple primitives without needing per-cipher-specific architecture tuning — a genuine tooling/generalization advance.
- **Related-key and multi-ciphertext-pair extensions** (numerous 2023–2025 papers per this report's search record — Lu et al.'s related-key differential-neural distinguishers for Simon/Simeck; multi-scale-convolutional-block architectures improving accuracy for Speck/Simon; a 2025 "More Insight" follow-up demonstrating that differential-neural attacks can outperform single-key results in the **related-key setting specifically**, achieving a 14-round key-recovery attack on Speck32/64 in that setting).
- **The 2025 RX-cryptanalysis-plus-deep-learning convergence** (2.4 above): explicitly combining differential-neural methodology with rotational-XOR difference structure, the most recent methodological combination in this line as of this report's writing.

**Requirements**: substantial training-data generation and GPU/ML-infrastructure investment, a genuinely different resource profile than classical automated-search cryptanalysis's CPU/MILP-solver-time profile — a real, practical consideration for an independent researcher weighing which attack-family expertise to build first (flagged again in `09`'s ranked-opportunities discussion). **Current research posture**: differential-neural cryptanalysis is now a mature, competitive, actively-published research line specifically against Speck/Simon/Simeck-family targets, not a novelty — any new ARX design proposal targeting genuine 2026-era rigor should, at minimum, be aware of this technique family even if not required to run a full neural-cryptanalysis evaluation against it (unlike MILP/SAT trail search, which — per `05` — genuinely is treated as mandatory evidence at top venues; differential-neural evaluation is not yet uniformly required, but is increasingly expected for designs closely related to Speck/Simon/ChaCha specifically, given how much published neural-cryptanalysis infrastructure already targets exactly those ciphers).
