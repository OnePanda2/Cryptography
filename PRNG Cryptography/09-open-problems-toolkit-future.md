## 2. Research Toolkit

### 2.1 Books and lecture notes

Beyond the general cryptography texts already covered in this conversation's earlier general-cryptography report (Part `06` §4) — Katz & Lindell, Boneh & Shoup, and the Handbook of Applied Cryptography remain directly relevant foundational reading — ARX-specific researchers should additionally prioritize: the **Sparkle/Alzette CRYPTO 2020 paper and its extended/full version** as the closest thing this sub-field has to a single "how to design a provably-margined ARX permutation" worked example (`02` §2.12); **university course materials specifically covering symmetric cryptanalysis** (several European university groups — the University of Luxembourg's SnT, KU Leuven's COSIC, Ruhr-University Bochum's HGI — maintain public lecture materials and are the academic homes of much of the research cited throughout this report, per the search record's institutional affiliations); and the **eSTREAM, CAESAR, and NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition final reports** (NIST IR 8268/8369/8454, per this conversation's general-cryptography report Part `05` §4's citation of the NIST LWC publication series) as the field's own authoritative, competition-scale comparative security/performance evaluations.

### 2.2 GitHub repositories and reference implementations

- **The Sparkle suite's own repositories** (`sparkle-lwc.github.io`, and the `cryptolu/sparkle` GitHub organization) — reference and optimized implementations, plus supporting analysis code, directly per `02` §2.12 and `07` §1.6.
- **CLAASP** (Bellini et al.) — the unified automated-analysis library covered in full in `05` §1.6, the single most directly actionable tool for a researcher wanting to define and evaluate a *new* ARX design.
- **CryptoSMT** (Stefan Kölbl) — the SAT/SMT-based cryptanalytic modeling framework covered in `05` §1.6/§2.3.
- **RustCrypto** — memory-safe Rust reference implementations of essentially every mainstream ARX design, useful both as implementation reference and as a starting point for a new design's own Rust implementation (`07` §1.5).
- **The Random123 (Threefry/Philox) reference repository** — the counter-based-PRNG reference implementation and documentation, directly relevant to `06` §1.1 and open problem #5 above.

### 2.3 MILP and SAT solvers

**Gurobi** and **CPLEX** (commercial, free academic licensing — the field's dominant MILP solvers, `05` §1.6); **CryptoMiniSat** (the standard cryptanalysis-oriented SAT solver, general-cryptography report Part `04` §1.4, equally standard here); **Google OR-Tools' CP-SAT** (`05` §2.2, an increasingly competitive alternative solver worth having in the toolkit alongside the MILP/pure-SAT options).

### 2.4 Visualization software and benchmark tools

Dedicated ARX-diffusion visualization tooling is less standardized/centralized than the MILP/SAT solver landscape — most published dependency-matrix heatmaps and avalanche-distribution plots in the current literature are generated via custom research-group scripting (commonly Python with matplotlib/seaborn, built directly on top of a reference implementation's output) rather than a single, field-standard visualization package; a researcher should expect to build this tooling themselves on top of their reference implementation, using the diffusion-metric definitions in `03` §2 directly as the specification for what to compute and plot. For benchmarking specifically, **SUPERCOP** (the eSTREAM/eBASH-lineage benchmarking framework) remains a widely-cited, standardized cross-implementation performance-measurement harness directly relevant to the reproducibility and methodology standards discussed in `07` §3.4.

### 2.5 Randomness testing suites

**PractRand**, **TestU01** (including BigCrush/SmallCrush/Rabbit), **Dieharder**, **NIST STS**, and **ENT** — all covered in full in `06` §2.1, all standard, expected, freely-available tools any new PRNG proposal should be run against as baseline (not sole) evidence.

### 2.6 Proof assistants

For the more formally-ambitious end of the security-evaluation spectrum (`05` §3, `08`'s expected-evidence checklist item on formal reductions): general-purpose proof assistants (Coq, Lean, Isabelle/HOL) are used in the broader cryptography formal-verification literature (general-cryptography report Part `05` §2.4's HACL* discussion is the most directly relevant example, F*-based rather than Coq/Lean-based specifically) for machine-checked security proofs, though this level of formal rigor remains the exception rather the expected baseline for a typical new-ARX-design paper as of 2026 — worth knowing exists and is increasingly encouraged at the field's frontier, without being a current hard requirement per `08`'s checklist.

### 2.7 Communities, mailing lists, conferences, and workshops

- **IACR's ePrint archive and its associated mailing-list/announcement channels** — the field's central, real-time preprint-and-discussion hub (`08` §3.1).
- **The CFRG (Crypto Forum Research Group)** and **NIST's public forums** (the lwc-forum specifically, per this report's search record showing active public technical discussion of Ascon's SP 800-232 draft, `02` §2.7) — worth monitoring directly for exactly the kind of live, working-level technical discussion (parameter choices, implementation-option tradeoffs) that doesn't always make it into the eventual published, polished paper.
- **CRYPTO, EUROCRYPT, ASIACRYPT, FSE/ToSC, CHES** — the conferences themselves (in-person and increasingly hybrid/recorded), covered in full in `08` §3.1; attending or watching recorded talks, even before having original research to present, is a standard, low-barrier way to build the field-specific intuition and current-awareness this entire report has tried to convey in written form.
- **University research groups actively publishing in this exact space** (per this report's search record's institutional affiliations): University of Luxembourg's SnT (Sparkle/Alzette's home institution), KU Leuven's COSIC, Ruhr-University Bochum's HGI, and numerous Chinese institutions producing a substantial share of the 2023–2025 automated-search and RX-cryptanalysis literature specifically (per the Executive Summary's explicit flag about this community's underrepresentation in casual "Western IACR venue" framing) — worth following directly via their public preprint output, not just their eventual formal-venue publications.

## 3. The Future of ARX

### 3.1 Current trends

Restating and synthesizing this report's fastest-moving threads (`04`, `05`, `06` §1.7, `09` §1) as the field's current live momentum: (a) **automated-search tooling itself is the most active current meta-trend** — CLAASP and the Window Heuristic (`05` §1.6) represent a shift from "each new cipher gets its own bespoke MILP model built from scratch" toward unified, reusable, define-once-analyze-automatically frameworks, a trend this report expects to continue and consolidate further; (b) **RX-cryptanalysis's continued tooling maturation** (`04` §2.4), extending its reach both to more rounds against established benchmark targets and to new attack-family combinations (the RX+rectangle, RX+differential-neural convergences already observed in the 2023–2025 literature); (c) **differential-neural cryptanalysis's continued maturation** from a novelty into a standard, expected part of the evaluation toolkit specifically for Speck/Simon/Simeck-class designs, with active, unresolved methodological questions (open problem #8 above) about how to properly interpret and weight its results relative to classical techniques.

### 3.2 Declining research areas

Pure, unautomated hand-derived trail search — once the field's only available methodology and the basis of essentially all pre-2011 ARX cryptanalysis — is now, per `05` §1.7's explicit statement, no longer considered adequate standalone evidence at a serious venue, and correspondingly declining as an independent research activity in its own right (though it remains pedagogically valuable, per `10`'s reading roadmap, for building the intuition needed to correctly interpret and sanity-check automated-search output). Similarly, isolated single-technique cryptanalysis papers (a pure differential attack with no differential-linear, RX, or automated-search-tooling engagement) are increasingly less competitive for publication at top venues relative to papers combining multiple techniques or contributing new methodology, per `08` §1.1 and §3.5's synthesis.

### 3.3 New cryptanalysis techniques and AI-assisted cryptanalysis

Beyond differential-neural cryptanalysis's now-established maturity (3.1 above), the frontier question — directly connecting to this conversation's earlier, separate general-cryptography report's coverage of the July 2026 Anthropic HAWK/reduced-round-AES event (that report's Part `03` §2.6 and Part `06` §1.7) — is **how far AI-assisted cryptanalysis generalizes beyond the current differential-neural-distinguisher paradigm specifically**. The HAWK/AES event is a different technique applied to different targets (a frontier AI model conducting largely autonomous mathematical research yielding a genuinely novel lattice-structure insight against a PQC signature scheme, and a meet-in-the-middle-technique improvement against reduced-round AES) than the ARX-specific differential-neural-distinguisher line this file has covered throughout (`04` §2.10) — but both sit within the same broader, live, unresolved question this report's Executive Summary and its companion general-cryptography report both flag explicitly: **as AI systems' capacity for sustained, semi-autonomous mathematical/cryptanalytic research grows, does it change how much scrutiny a "young" cryptographic design needs before the field trusts it, and does it change which attack techniques (beyond the already-established differential-neural-distinguisher paradigm) become newly tractable against ARX designs specifically?** This report's honest position, consistent with its companion report's treatment of the same underlying question: this is genuinely open, genuinely current (days old relative to this report's writing, in the HAWK/AES case specifically), and worth tracking directly via IACR ePrint and the specific research groups active in this space, rather than treated as settled in either direction.

### 3.4 AI-assisted design, formal methods, and automated design search

The natural design-side (not just cryptanalysis-side) counterpart to 3.3: using automated search — whether classical evolutionary/heuristic methods (`04` §1.2), MILP/SAT-guided optimization, or neural/ML-guided search — not just to *evaluate* a human-proposed design's security, but to directly *search* the ARX design space (round-function structure, rotation schedules, word-interconnection patterns) for candidates that jointly optimize security-margin (via an automated-search-derived fitness function) and performance (via a modeled or measured cycles-per-byte objective) simultaneously — a genuinely emerging, actively-discussed direction as of 2026, building directly on the rotation-constant-search methodology already covered in `04` §1.2, but extended to the *full* design space (not just rotation constants alone, given a fixed round structure) as the search target. **Constraint-guided optimization** and **neural guidance** specifically (using a trained model to guide/prioritize which regions of a large combinatorial design space to explore first, rather than exhaustive or purely-heuristic search) represent the most directly ML-adjacent version of this direction, conceptually paralleling how neural-network-guided search has been applied in other combinatorial-optimization domains, though — per this report's search record — genuinely novel-ARX-permutation design work of this fully-automated-design-search kind is thinner in the current published literature than either the automated-*cryptanalysis* tooling (`05`) or the differential-neural-*attack* line (`04` §2.10), making this a real, currently-underexplored frontier rather than an established practice, and a natural, direct extension of open problem #7 above for a researcher specifically interested in the AI-assisted-design angle.

### 3.5 Where the field is likely to move over the next decade

Synthesizing this file's trends into a forward-looking, explicitly-labeled-as-uncertain projection: continued consolidation around unified, reusable automated-analysis tooling (CLAASP-style frameworks becoming more capable and more standard, potentially eventually approaching a "define your cipher, get a full standardized security report" workflow that doesn't yet fully exist); continued, incremental cryptanalytic progress against ChaCha/Speck/Simon-class established targets via the differential-linear/RX/differential-neural technique combinations already underway, likely continuing to creep forward by fractional rounds per year in the ChaCha case specifically without threatening deployed full-round security within any foreseeable horizon; growing, though currently still uncertain-in-scope, AI-assisted-cryptanalysis capability specifically (3.3 above), which this report — consistent with its companion general-cryptography report's treatment of the same underlying question — declines to confidently project further than "genuinely worth tracking, not yet a settled trajectory"; and a plausible, if currently underexplored, growth in fully-automated ARX design-search research (3.4 above) as the natural next step once the cryptanalysis-evaluation tooling (already comparatively mature, per `05`) is more routinely paired with an equally rigorous, automated design-*generation* search rather than remaining primarily a post-hoc evaluation tool applied to human-proposed designs.
