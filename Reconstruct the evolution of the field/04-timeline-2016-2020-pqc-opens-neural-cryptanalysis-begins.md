# 04 — 2016–2020: PQC Opens, and Cryptanalysis Learns to Learn

*Part of a multi-file chronological history — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map and methodology.*

---

## The state of the field entering 2016

Post-Snowden provenance-consciousness was now fully baked into the field's norms; MILP-based automated cryptanalysis was five years old and growing; Curve25519/Ed25519 adoption was accelerating; and Shor's algorithm, twenty-two years old, was about to stop being purely theoretical in its practical urgency, for reasons this chapter traces in detail.

## 2016 — NIST opens PQC standardization, and RX-cryptanalysis is founded

**NIST formally opened its Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardization process**, announced at the PQCrypto 2016 conference, calling for submissions across every major PQC hard-problem family (lattices, codes, hash-based, isogenies, multivariate). **Why now, twenty-two years after Shor's algorithm**: this wasn't a sudden discovery — it reflects the field having spent the intervening two decades doing the *prerequisite* mathematical groundwork (Regev's Learning With Errors and its worst-case-to-average-case reduction, 2005; NTRU, 1996; McEliece's code-based scheme, dating all the way back to 1978 and newly relevant again) to the point where genuinely serious, well-analyzed *candidate replacements* for RSA/ECC finally existed in sufficient number and maturity to make an open competition — the same institutional template used for AES and SHA-3 — worth running. **Why an open competition specifically, rather than NIST simply picking the best-understood option (lattices) directly**: the post-Snowden trust environment (Part `03`) made a closed or fast-tracked selection process politically and technically untenable — the field needed, and NIST needed to be seen providing, the same multi-round, globally-scrutinized process that had worked for AES and SHA-3, especially for a decision this consequential and this certain to eventually touch nearly every secured system on the internet.

Separately, **Ashur and Liu published the founding rotational-XOR (RX) cryptanalysis paper** at FSE, generalizing the older rotational-cryptanalysis idea (Khovratovich and Nikolić, 2010) to correctly handle round-constant injection — a technique that would remain a comparatively narrow, specialist tool for the following six-plus years before maturing into a fully-automated, tooled, mainstream attack class in the 2023–2025 window (Part `05`).

## 2017 — SHA-1 finally, practically falls

**Google and CWI Amsterdam's "SHAttered" attack** produced the first actual, constructed SHA-1 collision — two distinct PDF files with identical SHA-1 hashes — twelve years after Wang, Yin, and Yu's 2005 *theoretical* collision-finding result had first broken confidence in the algorithm. **Why the twelve-year gap between theoretical and practical breaks matters as a historical pattern**: this is a recurring rhythm in this history — a theoretical weakness is published, the field treats it as a serious warning and begins migrating away, and the *practical* demonstration, when it eventually arrives, mostly confirms what informed practitioners already believed rather than triggering fresh panic; SHA-1 had already been substantially phased out of new deployments and distrusted by security-conscious practitioners well before SHAttered, making the practical break a confirmation and a forcing function for remaining laggards, not a genuine surprise to the field's expert consensus.

## 2018 — TLS 1.3 finalizes, and ISO rejects Speck/Simon

**RFC 8446 (TLS 1.3)** was finalized, closing a four-year drafting process (begun 2014, Part `03`) and delivering the protocol's headline improvements: mandatory forward secrecy for every handshake, removal of static RSA key exchange and CBC-mode ciphersuites, a reduced one-round-trip handshake, and — for the first time as a standardized, first-class default option — **ChaCha20-Poly1305** alongside AES-GCM, a direct institutional validation of the ARX/AEAD lineage traced through this entire history, thirteen years after Salsa20's original 2005 side-channel-motivated debut.

**ISO/IEC's lightweight-cryptography standardization process rejected Speck and Simon** for inclusion in ISO/IEC 29192, reportedly over unresolved transparency concerns about the design and security-analysis process — despite, as this history has emphasized, no cryptanalytic weakness having ever been found against the full-round ciphers. This is the direct, dated payoff of the provenance dynamic set in motion by 2013's Snowden disclosures: five years later, an NSA-designed cipher family with an entirely clean cryptanalytic track record was still institutionally rejected on trust grounds alone — the clearest possible illustration that, by this point in the field's history, mathematics and institutional trust had become genuinely, formally separable evaluation axes, and a design needed to clear both bars, not just one.

**The NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition** also opened this year, seeking a dedicated standard for resource-constrained (IoT, embedded) devices — the fourth major NIST open competition this history has now traced (after AES, SHA-3, and the still-ongoing PQC process), and the one that would eventually produce the Ascon/Sparkle/Xoodyak/TinyJambu comparative case study central to this report's companion ARX-specific document.

## 2019 — Gohr teaches a neural network to do cryptanalysis

**Aron Gohr published "Improving Attacks on Round-Reduced Speck32/64 Using Deep Learning"** at CRYPTO — training a neural network directly as a differential distinguisher against a real, well-studied benchmark cipher, and demonstrating it could match or exceed the distinguishing power of hand-derived classical differential-distribution-table-based approaches, then using the trained distinguisher inside a genuine key-recovery attack pipeline with results competitive with the era's best classical attacks. **Why this was a genuine field-changing moment, not a novelty**: this is the first time a machine-learning technique demonstrably matched serious, expert-level cryptanalytic craft on a real benchmark target, not a toy problem — and it opened a research line (differential-neural cryptanalysis) that would mature into a fully competitive, actively-published sub-field over the following six years (Part `05`), and stands, in the long view this report takes, as the direct conceptual ancestor of the far more dramatic AI-cryptanalysis event that would close out this entire 33-year history in July 2026.

## 2020 — Sparkle/Alzette proves ARX can be provably margined

**Beierle, Biryukov, Cardoso dos Santos, Großschädl, Perrin, Udovenko, Velichkov, and Wang published the Sparkle permutation family and its Alzette ARX-box** at CRYPTO, introducing the **long-trail strategy** — a purpose-built, ARX-specific adaptation of AES's wide-trail strategy, giving an ARX design, for the first time, a *provable* (not merely empirically-tested) bound on the best possible differential/linear characteristic probability. **Why this mattered, and why it took nineteen years after AES's wide-trail strategy (2001) to arrive**: adapting the wide-trail strategy's "count active S-boxes" argument to ARX required real, separate research work, because modular addition's carry-chain-dependent nonlinearity doesn't have the same simple, small, exhaustively-verifiable table an S-box's differential distribution function gives for free — establishing Alzette's own per-instance probability bound needed its own dedicated automated-search effort, building directly on the MILP/SAT tooling lineage traced from 2011 onward in this history. Sparkle became a NIST Lightweight Cryptography competition finalist (though ultimately not the winner, per Part `05`).

In the same year, **Beierle, Leander, and Todo published "Improved Differential-Linear Attacks with Applications to ARX Ciphers"** at CRYPTO, introducing a refined 3.5-round differential specifically improving ChaCha/Salsa20 cryptanalysis — the opening move in what would become, across the following five years (Part `05`), a steady, incremental, MILP-and-later-bit-puncturing-boosted advance pushing ChaCha's best-known attack from roughly 7 rounds toward 7.5 of its full 20, without ever coming close to threatening the deployed cipher.

## What this era established, looking forward

By the end of 2020, the field had: opened the post-quantum standardization process that would, over the following four years, produce the field's first finalized quantum-resistant standards; watched SHA-1's theoretical 2005 break finally, practically materialize thirteen years later, confirming a now-familiar theoretical-warning-then-practical-confirmation rhythm; watched TLS 1.3 institutionally validate ARX/ChaCha20-Poly1305 as first-class, standardized internet infrastructure; watched the provenance-trust dynamic ignited by Snowden in 2013 concretely cost an entire, cryptanalytically-clean cipher family (Speck/Simon) formal international standardization; gained a second major automated-cryptanalysis paradigm (differential-neural distinguishers, via Gohr) to sit alongside MILP/SAT trail search; and watched ARX achieve, via Sparkle/Alzette's long-trail strategy, the same category of provable security margin AES had achieved nineteen years earlier — closing, in a real sense, the last major methodological gap between ARX design and SPN design's rigor. Part `05`, the final chronological chapter, covers the standardization of everything set in motion here — PQC's finalization, Ascon's win, and the AI-cryptanalysis event that closes this 33-year history on a genuinely unresolved, still-unfolding note.
