# 02 — 2002–2008: The Hash-Function Crisis and ARX's Arrival

*Part of a multi-file chronological history — see `00-INDEX-executive-summary.md` for the map and methodology.*

---

## The state of the field entering 2002

AES had just been finalized and confidence in it was, correctly, high and durable. The field's comfortable assumption going into this era was that **hash functions were a solved problem** — MD5 and SHA-1, both Merkle–Damgård/Davies–Meyer designs from the same intellectual lineage, were deployed everywhere (certificate signing, integrity checking, password storage in far too many places), and while MD5 had accumulated some theoretical warning signs through the 1990s, nothing had yet demonstrated a practical break. This confidence would not survive the decade.

## 2002–2003 — Warning signs accumulate

Theoretical weaknesses in MD5's compression function continued to be published through this period, and cryptographers working closely on hash function design (including Daemen and Rijmen, fresh off AES) were already privately and publicly voicing unease about the entire Merkle–Damgård family's structural properties — particularly its **length-extension** weakness (given H(m), one can compute H(m ‖ pad ‖ m') without knowing m), a property that wasn't yet the subject of a headline attack but was well understood as a structural wart. This period is best understood as the quiet buildup before the break, not yet a crisis — but the unease was specific and well-founded enough that it directly shaped design thinking that would surface later in the decade (the sponge construction, conceived partly as an explicit response to exactly this length-extension weakness).

## 2004 — MD5 falls, and the ROM's theoretical ceiling is proven

Two, almost unrelated, foundational events happened in the same year, and both reshaped the field's trajectory.

**Xiaoyun Wang, Dengguo Feng, Xuejia Lai, and Hongbo Yu published practical MD5 collisions** — not a theoretical weakness anymore, but actual, constructible pairs of distinct messages hashing to the same value, found via a differential-cryptanalysis-style approach adapted specifically to the hash-function setting. This was the moment the field's "hash functions are solved" complacency broke. **Why this mattered immediately and viscerally**: MD5 wasn't a niche or legacy algorithm — it was in active, heavy, security-critical use, and a practical collision meant an adversary could, with real effort, construct two different documents/certificates/files with identical MD5 hashes, directly undermining every application relying on MD5's collision resistance.

Separately, and more quietly at the time but arguably just as consequential in the long run, **Canetti, Goldreich, and Halevi published "The Random Oracle Methodology, Revisited"** (initially presented at STOC 1998, but the fuller journal treatment and the result's absorption into mainstream field consciousness is generally dated to this broader early-2000s window, with 2004 as the commonly-cited journal-publication year) proving that schemes exist which are secure in the idealized random oracle model but **provably insecure for every possible real-world hash function instantiation** — a genuine, rigorous demonstration that ROM security is not a guarantee of real-world security, only strong heuristic evidence. **Why this didn't kill ROM-based design**: the constructions Canetti-Goldreich-Halevi built to prove their point were deliberately, artificially pathological — no one believed *practical* schemes like OAEP or PSS were secretly hiding this kind of flaw — but the proof meant the field could no longer treat "secure in the ROM" as a mathematically airtight guarantee; it became explicitly understood field-wide as strong evidence, not proof, a distinction the field has maintained precisely and consistently ever since.

## 2005 — SHA-1 cracks, and Bernstein answers a side-channel problem with a new cipher

**Wang, Yin, and Yu extended their MD5 attack methodology to SHA-1**, publishing a theoretical collision-finding attack requiring around 2^69 operations — well below the 2^80 brute-force birthday bound SHA-1's 160-bit output was supposed to guarantee, though still far beyond what was practically executable in 2005. This didn't break SHA-1 *practically* yet (that was twelve years away, Part `03`), but it broke the *theoretical* confidence the field had in it, and — crucially — it broke confidence in the entire structural family (Merkle–Damgård/Davies–Meyer) that MD5, SHA-1, *and* SHA-2 all shared, raising a genuinely uncomfortable question: if the attack methodology generalizes across the family, is SHA-2 next?

In parallel, and largely independently motivated, **Daniel J. Bernstein published Salsa20**, submitted to the newly-launched eSTREAM competition (see below). The direct, explicitly-stated motivation was **software AES's cache-timing vulnerability** — table-lookup-based AES implementations, the standard approach before dedicated hardware existed, leaked key-dependent information through measurable cache-access-pattern timing, a real, already-demonstrated attack class (Bernstein's own "Cache-timing attacks on AES," also 2005) with no good software-only fix available at acceptable performance cost. Salsa20's answer — build the entire cipher from addition, rotation, and XOR, with **no table lookups at all**, hence no secret-dependent memory-access pattern to leak — is the moment the ARX paradigm, in its modern, deliberately-side-channel-motivated form, actually arrives as a named, principled design philosophy, not merely an incidental operation choice (as RC5's data-dependent rotations had been in 1994).

**Why the timing mattered**: this is five years before AES-NI hardware acceleration (2010) would make the cache-timing problem largely moot for anyone with new-enough silicon. In 2005, there was a real, live, unsolved problem for software AES on general-purpose hardware, and Salsa20 was a direct, dated response to it — not a solution arriving after the problem had already been fixed some other way.

## 2006 — Curve25519 and Dual_EC_DRBG, on a collision course

**Bernstein published "Curve25519: new Diffie-Hellman speed records,"** introducing a Montgomery-form elliptic curve specifically engineered for speed, implementation-safety (avoiding entire classes of point-validation and exceptional-case implementation bugs that plague general Weierstrass-form curve arithmetic), and — significantly for later history — **fully reproducible, "nothing up my sleeve" parameter generation**. At the time this was a performance and implementation-safety contribution first and foremost; its provenance-transparency property wouldn't become the *dominant* reason for its eventual adoption until the Dual_EC/Snowden episode (Part `03`) made curve provenance a live trust issue years later.

In the same year, **NIST standardized Dual_EC_DRBG** (SP 800-90A) as one of several approved deterministic random-bit-generator mechanisms — an elliptic-curve-based design, structurally unusual among DRBGs for resting on number-theoretic rather than symmetric-primitive hardness. Nothing about its standardization in 2006 raised alarm; the mathematical property that would make it dangerous hadn't yet been publicly identified.

## 2007 — The SHA-3 competition opens, and Dual_EC's flaw surfaces

Directly responding to 2005's SHA-1 theoretical break and the broader Merkle–Damgård-family unease building since 2002–2003, **NIST opened the SHA-3 competition**, explicitly modeled on AES's open, multi-round, publicly-cryptanalyzed process — the second major reuse of that institutional template, and a deliberate, explicit hedge: SHA-2 itself hadn't been broken, but the field judged a structurally *different* backup worth developing before, not after, SHA-2 failed (it never did fail, as it happens, but the hedge was judged prudent regardless, and this "hedge before failure, not after" instinct recurs directly in the PQC program's HQC/SLH-DSA diversification a decade and a half later, Part `05`).

Separately, **Dan Shumow and Niels Ferguson publicly demonstrated that Dual_EC_DRBG's design permits a backdoor**: if the specific constant elliptic-curve points defining the algorithm were chosen (by whoever generated them) with a known secret relationship, that party could predict all future generator outputs from a small number of observed outputs. This was, at the time, a *theoretical* vulnerability — no one had proven the actual standardized constants *were* backdoored, only that the design permitted it — but it planted a specific, dated, public warning that would be dramatically substantiated six years later (Part `03`).

## 2008 — ChaCha refines Salsa20, and NORX-adjacent duplex thinking begins

**Bernstein published ChaCha**, an explicit refinement of Salsa20 aimed at improving diffusion-per-round without sacrificing performance — swapping Salsa20's "column round then row round" alternation for ChaCha's "column round then diagonal round" pattern, achieving faster full-state mixing for the same operation count. This is a genuinely important, if narrow, design-methodology moment: ChaCha wasn't a reaction to any *attack* on Salsa20 (none existed, or exists as of 2026, against the full cipher) — it was a voluntary, analysis-driven refinement, the ARX paradigm's design community iterating on itself based on diffusion-rate reasoning alone, a pattern of continuous, non-crisis-driven refinement quite different from the attack-driven design changes dominating the hash-function story this same year.

Also in 2008, in the SHA-3 competition's first-round submission pool, **early sponge-construction thinking (Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters, Van Assche's Keccak submission, and the broader theoretical indifferentiability work that would be published fully the same year)** began establishing the sponge as a genuinely structurally different alternative to Merkle–Damgård — absorbing input and squeezing output via repeated application of a fixed permutation to a large internal state, with **no length-extension weakness by construction**, directly answering the structural wart that had worried the field since 2002–2003.

---

## What this era established, looking forward

By the end of 2008, the field had: watched its most heavily-deployed hash function (MD5) fall completely and its second-most-heavily-deployed one (SHA-1) fall theoretically, in both cases via cryptanalytic techniques the *field itself* had developed and openly published (Wang et al.'s work, building on open differential-cryptanalysis methodology, not classified insight); learned, rigorously, that "secure in the random oracle model" is strong evidence, not proof; watched ARX arrive as a deliberate, side-channel-motivated design philosophy with two now-canonical instances (Salsa20, ChaCha) whose full-round security has still not meaningfully eroded eighteen years later; planted a specific, public, prescient warning about Dual_EC_DRBG's backdoor potential that the field would not fully reckon with for another six years; and opened a second AES-style open competition (SHA-3), whose eventual winner (Keccak) would introduce the sponge construction as a durable, field-changing structural paradigm. Part `03` picks up with that competition's conclusion and the Dual_EC story's dramatic, Snowden-driven resolution.
